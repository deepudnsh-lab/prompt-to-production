01 — Why this persona exists

Every design decision
needs a person
to answer to.

A fine dining landing page is not designed for everyone. It is designed for a specific person with a specific relationship to food, money, experience, and digital behaviour. Without a clear persona, design decisions default to personal taste or generic best practices — neither of which produces work that converts browsers into reservation holders.

The persona below is constructed from real data about the primary demographic driving India's fine dining growth, cross-referenced with global research on upscale diner behaviour. The fictional name and narrative details are a device for making the profile usable in practice — every demographic and behavioural attribute is grounded in cited research.

Data foundation: 70% of frequent fine dining guests are aged 35–54 (Restroworks / IMARC, 2025). A growing base of affluent millennials and urban professionals is choosing premium dining as a lifestyle expression rather than an occasional indulgence (Restaurant India, 2026). Urban Indians now dine out around 5 times a month, expected to reach 7–8 times by 2030, with Gen Z driving 40% of consumption (Restroworks, 2025). The persona draws on these patterns and sits at their intersection.

03 — Who Rohit actually is

The person behind
the profile.

Rohit grew up in a middle-class family in Delhi. He moved to Bangalore for his first job, did his MBA, and has spent the last decade building a career in product leadership. He earns well now — more than he ever expected to — and food has become one of the primary ways he and his wife Priya celebrate, unwind, and signal that they have arrived somewhere in life.

He is not a food snob in the Instagram influencer sense. He doesn't post every meal. But he pays attention — he notices when a restaurant uses the wrong glass for a red wine, when the pacing between courses is off, when a server doesn't know the provenance of the ingredients. These are the details that make him feel a restaurant is taking the experience seriously, and he is willing to pay a meaningful premium for a place that gets it right.

Rohit uses fine dining for three distinct occasions: client entertainment (he needs a place that will impress a visiting stakeholder), date nights with Priya (a ritual they protect fiercely despite their schedules), and personal milestone celebrations. He plans ahead for all three. He is not a spontaneous fine dining visitor — he books days, sometimes weeks, in advance. The website is where he makes his first assessment of whether a place is worth his time.

He has been to restaurants in London, Tokyo, and New York. He knows what a well-designed fine dining website looks like. A site that feels generic, slow, or visually cheap immediately registers as a signal that the food and service might not be held to a higher standard either. Conversely, a site that feels premium, unhurried, and specific — that gives him a real sense of the philosophy behind the food — creates immediate credibility and a genuine desire to book.

04 — Digital & dining behaviours

How Rohit actually
finds and books
a restaurant.

01
Discovery starts with a recommendation, not a search
Rohit typically hears about a restaurant from a colleague, a food writer he follows on Instagram, or a review in a publication like Condé Nast Traveller India. He rarely discovers restaurants through Google Search — he uses Google to verify a recommendation, not to initiate one.
02
The website is the final assessment before booking
Once he has a recommendation, the website is where the decision is made. He spends 2–4 minutes on average. He looks at: photography quality (signals how seriously they take presentation), the menu (does it have a point of view?), and the reservation system (friction here is a deal-breaker). If the site doesn't load well on mobile, he moves on.
03
Mobile-first browsing, desktop for booking decisions
He typically encounters the website first on his phone — during a commute or between meetings. But he often revisits on a desktop or laptop before making the actual reservation, especially if the occasion is important or the spend is significant. The mobile experience must impress; the desktop experience must convert.
04
He reads the menu before anything else
The menu is the first thing he looks for. Not to check prices — to assess whether the restaurant has a culinary philosophy. A menu that reads like a generic Indian fine dining template (paneer tikka with truffle, standard grilled options) tells him the kitchen is not thinking hard enough. A menu that references specific techniques, wood types, or seasonal sourcing creates immediate credibility.
05
He books in advance and plans around it
Rohit does not make spontaneous fine dining bookings. For date nights, he plans 1–2 weeks ahead. For client dinners, 2–3 weeks. For special occasions, sometimes a month in advance. He respects a restaurant that is hard to book — scarcity signals quality in his mental model. A reservation form that communicates "limited availability" performs better than one that implies instant access.
06
He shares experiences, but selectively
He is not an influencer. He shares an exceptional meal with a small circle — a photo to a friends WhatsApp group, a recommendation in a Slack channel, a mention to a colleague. This is the word-of-mouth chain that fine dining depends on. A memorable website and experience makes him more likely to share the recommendation; a generic one makes him less likely to feel it is worth sharing.
05 — Pains & motivations

What frustrates him.
What moves him.

Pain points when visiting fine dining websites
  • Websites that look like generic restaurant templates — dark background, italicised quote, hero image of steak. No personality, no differentiation.
  • PDF menus. Having to download a file to see what's on the menu feels like a step backwards from every other considered digital experience in his life.
  • Reservation systems that ask for too much information upfront, or redirect to a third-party platform (Zomato, Dineout) for a premium restaurant — it breaks the premium experience immediately.
  • Slow-loading sites. He has a fast phone and fast internet. A site that takes more than 3 seconds registers as unprofessional and raises questions about operational attention to detail.
  • Copy that tells him the restaurant is "the best" or "unparalleled" without showing him why. He is a product person — he values specifics over superlatives.
What motivates a booking decision
  • A specific, named philosophy behind the food. Not "seasonal ingredients" — "hickory-fired lamb, apple-smoked sea bass." The specificity signals a kitchen that makes deliberate decisions.
  • Visible scarcity — knowing that only 28 covers are served each evening makes him feel the experience is curated, not mass-produced. He wants to feel that getting a table is an achievement.
  • A site that communicates the experience before the food — the atmosphere, the pacing, the ritual. He is buying an evening, not just a meal.
  • A chef with a visible point of view. He wants to know who is responsible for the food and what they believe in — even on a hypothetical brand, the chef's philosophy should be legible from the website.
  • A reservation process that feels effortless and immediate. He has limited time and a low tolerance for friction. A clean, simple form that confirms quickly moves him from intent to booking faster than anything else.

07 — The single most important question

What would make
Rohit trust Ember &
Oak instantly?

This is the defining question for the entire design. Every heading, every image decision, every section of copy should be answerable by asking whether it contributes to this moment.

The trust moment · Ember & Oak
"A site that shows me, in the first ten seconds, that someone made deliberate decisions about every detail — the wood, the fire, the cover count, the pacing — earns my trust before I've read a single word of copy."
Visual signal
A dark, atmospheric hero that feels composed rather than assembled. The quality of the typography and the warmth of the ember tones communicates premium intent before any text is read.
Narrative signal
Specific language about the method — "hickory for the lamb, apple for the duck" — tells Rohit immediately that the kitchen makes intentional decisions. Specificity earns trust where superlatives do not.
Functional signal
A reservation system that is visible, clean, and immediate. The ability to book without leaving the page or being redirected to a third-party platform signals that the restaurant controls its own experience end to end.
08 — Design implications

What this persona
means for every
design decision.

The following implications are derived directly from Rohit's profile, behaviours, and trust triggers. Each one will be applied in the moodboard, copy, and layout concept stages that follow.

01 The hero must communicate philosophy, not just atmosphere
Rohit's trust is earned by specificity, not beauty. The hero section cannot be a beautiful dark image and a vague headline. It needs to tell him something precise about what Ember & Oak is — the fire, the wood, the ritual — within the first viewport.
02 The menu section must read as a designed document, not a list
Rohit reads the menu before everything else. It needs to feel considered and visually structured — not a bulleted list or a grid of prices. The wood type used for each dish, the seasonal sourcing note, the pairing suggestion — these details signal a kitchen that thinks.
03 Scarcity must be made explicit and visual
"Only 28 guests each evening" should appear prominently — in the hero strip, in the reservation section, or both. For Rohit, this is not a deterrent. It is a confirmation that the experience is curated and that getting a table means something.
04 Mobile performance is non-negotiable
Rohit's first encounter with the site is almost always on his phone. A site that is slow, poorly scaled, or loses its visual quality at mobile viewport will lose him before he ever opens a laptop to make the booking. The design must be tested at mobile first.
05 Copy must be specific, not superlative
Rohit is a product person. He processes specifics faster and trusts them more than claims. "The best fine dining experience in Bangalore" tells him nothing. "Fire lit at four. Hickory for the lamb. Only 28 guests." tells him everything. Every headline in the copy phase must be tested against this principle.
06 The reservation form must be clean and on-page
No third-party redirects. No multi-step form that asks for dietary requirements before he has even confirmed he wants to book. A simple, elegant form — party size, date, seating preference, contact — that confirms immediately and lives on the page. Friction here is the single biggest conversion killer for this persona.
