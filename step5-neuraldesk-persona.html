Marcus is a Product Manager at a Series B SaaS company in Austin, Texas. He owns the product metrics — activation rates, retention curves, feature adoption, churn signals. He needs to bring data to every sprint review, every investor update, every cross-functional meeting. He has one data analyst on his team who is always at capacity and a two-day SLA on any custom report request.

Marcus is not a data engineer. He can read a pivot table and write a basic formula. He has tried Google Sheets, Looker Studio, and a frustrating week with Power BI. What he needs is a tool that lets him ask a question in plain English and get a chart he can drop into a slide deck without having to clean it first.

The buyer thesis: Marcus does not buy data software. He buys back three hours of his week — and the confidence to walk into a meeting with the answer already in his hand.

02 — Six defining behaviours

How Marcus
actually works.

B01
He discovers on LinkedIn and Product Hunt, not Google Search
Marcus's tool discovery happens through his professional network. A PM peer posts about a tool that saved them hours. A Product Hunt launch catches his eye on a Tuesday morning. He rarely searches "AI analytics tool" — he finds tools through social proof in communities he already trusts.
B02
He evaluates three tools simultaneously in three tabs
Marcus opens three tabs — NeuralDesk, a competitor, and a Reddit thread comparing both. The landing page that wins is the one that communicates its value fastest, because he is context-switching constantly. He will not scroll past the fold if the headline did not already earn his next 30 seconds.
B03
He starts the trial before reading the documentation
Marcus's evaluation process is hands-on. He signs up, connects his Google Sheets data, and tries to answer a real question he actually needs answered. If the product cannot get him to a result in under 10 minutes, he closes the tab. Documentation, onboarding videos, and tutorial emails are ignored until after the first successful output.
B04
He is the decision-maker and the approval-seeker simultaneously
Tools under $200/month are Marcus's solo call. But anything above that requires a Slack message to his manager with a "here's why" summary. The landing page must give him the ammunition to make that Slack message convincing — a clear ROI case, specific time-saving claims, recognisable company logos in the trust strip.
B05
He shares outputs publicly — his Slack and email are his proof of work
Every chart Marcus generates that goes into a slide deck or a Slack message is an implicit endorsement of the tool that produced it. If the output looks polished and professional, he becomes an organic advocate — forwarding the chart and mentioning what tool made it. The quality of the output is a distribution channel.
B06
He is deeply skeptical of "AI" as a feature claim
Marcus has been burned before — tools that promised AI-powered insights and delivered rebranded pivot tables. He does not want to be told the product uses AI. He wants to see it work. The most convincing claim is a specific, verifiable outcome: "reduced reporting time from 3 days to 60 seconds." Not "our AI gives you powerful insights."

04 — Trust signals vs friction points

What converts Marcus.
What loses him.

✓ Trust signals — what converts
Named company logos above the fold — not "500+ companies." Logos of brands Marcus recognises tell him the credibility question is already answered.
Specific time-saving metrics — "Reduced weekly reporting from 4 hours to 20 minutes." Not "saves you time." The specificity is the proof.
Free tier or free trial with no credit card required — removes the risk entirely. Marcus will not enter payment details before seeing a result.
Google OAuth sign-up — one click. Any form with more than name + email + password loses him at the third field.
SOC 2 / GDPR badge visible — Marcus's company has a data security policy. He needs to see the certifications before he connects real company data.
Pricing on the page — with plan comparison. He wants to know before he invests 20 minutes in a trial whether he can actually pay for it.
✕ Friction points — what loses him
"Contact sales for pricing" — immediate exit. If Marcus cannot see the price, he assumes it is out of budget and tabs over to the competitor.
Vague AI claims — "Powerful AI insights." "Next-generation analytics." "Intelligent reporting." These are red flags, not selling points. Marcus has been burned by these before.
Tutorial required before the product — any mandatory onboarding video, demo call, or walkthrough before he can connect data and run a query will lose him. He needs to touch the product first.
Jargon in the hero section — "Connect your data warehouse." "Query your Snowflake instance." "Build a BI pipeline." Marcus does not have a data warehouse. He has a Google Sheet.
Ugly output quality — if the chart the product generates looks like a default Excel chart, Marcus will not send it to his manager. The output quality is the product demo. It must be share-ready.
Generic stock photography — a smiling person pointing at a monitor is the visual equivalent of "powerful AI insights." It signals that the brand has not thought carefully about who it is speaking to.
05 — Six design implications

What this means
for NeuralDesk.

D01
Hero headline leads with outcome, not product description
"Your answer in 60 seconds" — not "AI-powered data analytics platform." Marcus decides in the first 5 seconds whether to keep reading. The headline must describe the result he gets, not the technology that produces it.
D02
Logo wall in the first viewport — before any feature explanation
Named company logos immediately below the hero. Not "trusted by 500+ teams" — actual logos. This answers the credibility question before Marcus has to ask it, and lets him continue scrolling rather than tabbing to a competitor.
D03
Natural language query demonstration at hero level
Show a prompt field with a real question typed into it — "What was my user activation rate last month by acquisition channel?" — and the chart it produced. This is more persuasive than any feature list because it shows Marcus exactly what he would type and exactly what he would get back.
D04
Pricing visible on the homepage with a free tier clearly labelled
Three tiers: Free (no credit card), Pro ($29/month), Team ($79/month). The free tier removes every conversion barrier. The Pro price is within Marcus's solo approval limit. The Team price has a "ask your manager" button that generates a pre-written justification email.
D05
Output quality is non-negotiable — charts must be share-ready
Every chart NeuralDesk generates must be polished enough to drop directly into a slide deck or a Slack message without editing. Font, colour, axis labels, and layout must be professional. The output quality is Marcus's proof of work — and NeuralDesk's primary distribution channel.
D06
Specific testimonials with named outcomes — no generic praise
"We reduced our weekly reporting time from 4 hours to 20 minutes in the first week." — Priya S., Product Manager, Stripe. Every testimonial must have a name, a title, a company, and a specific metric. Generic testimonials ("Great tool! Highly recommend.") have negative credibility with Marcus — they signal that real outcomes are unavailable.
