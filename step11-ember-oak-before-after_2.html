01 — What this comparison shows

The gap between
average and intentional.

The "before" in this document is not a real website. It is a composite of the visual and structural patterns observed across the majority of fine dining websites in India — the category average identified in the Step 6 competitor audit. It represents what most fine dining sites look like when no one has made deliberate decisions about the design.

The "after" is Ember & Oak — the result of 10 steps of research, persona development, moodboarding, copywriting, and deliberate design direction. Every difference between the two is the product of a specific decision made at a specific step in this process.

How to read this document: Each comparison section shows a simulated screen of the before and after side by side, followed by an annotation table explaining what changed and which step of the process drove each change. The before uses real patterns from the category audit. The after uses the actual copy, typography, and palette from the Ember & Oak landing page.

Element Before After The decision · which step drove it
Headline "An Unforgettable Culinary Experience" "Where fire meets flavour." Step 08 copy rule: specific beats superlative. "Unforgettable" is a claim. "Where fire meets flavour" is a fact about the cooking method and a brand identity statement simultaneously.
CTA button text Book Now Reserve a Table Step 08 copy rule: "Reserve" is quieter and more considered than "Book." It implies the table is already set — the guest is claiming it, not placing an order. Applied consistently across every CTA on the page.
Scarcity signal Absent 28 guests each evening · in eyebrow Step 06 audit finding: scarcity is the gap no competitor closes. Surfaced at the first viewport as factual information, not as a pressure tactic. From Step 05 persona: Rohit responds to exclusivity signals when they are stated as fact.
Category tags Fine Dining · Multi-Cuisine · Live Music Hickory for the lamb · Apple for the sea bass Step 04 research insight: method storytelling is the gap the category leaves open. The wood strip replaces generic category tags with specific, named cooking decisions — communicating craft in the same visual space.
Background palette Warm beige gradient Near-black #0E0A06 Step 07 moodboard: Direction A (Noir) selected over B and C. Dark mode is the established visual register of premium fine dining globally. Ember orange as the single accent creates warmth within darkness — the gap EMP and Canlis both leave open.
Typography Default serif · no considered pairing Cormorant Garamond Light 300 + DM Mono Step 07 naming selection: Cormorant Garamond at Light 300 creates editorial tension — refined and slightly fragile at display scale, mirroring the experience of food that takes hours to make and minutes to eat. DM Mono for all labels.

Element Before After The decision · which step drove it
Menu format PDF download link Native HTML · inline Step 04 research: PDF menus are a 2015-era UX failure. They cannot be indexed by search engines, do not resize for mobile, and interrupt the browsing flow. Step 05 persona: Rohit uses mobile first — a PDF download would lose him immediately.
Wood type label Absent Named per dish · right-aligned tag Step 08 copy rule: the wood is always named. Step 04 insight: method storytelling is the visual gap no competitor closes. The wood tag on every dish makes the philosophy section above feel earned rather than promotional.
Ingredient sourcing Generic: "Imported Ribeye" "seasonal veg" Specific: "Nilgiri Sea Bass" "Deccan Lamb" Step 08 copy: Indian sourcing references localise the menu without making it feel regional. They signal provenance and quality simultaneously — the kind of specificity Rohit reads as depth of craft.
Seasonal signal Absent "Spring 2026" · "The menu changes when the season does. Not before." Step 08 copy: the seasonal label makes the commitment to freshness visible and specific. The closing line — "Not before" — is a statement of operating principle in two words. It signals that seasons, not business convenience, drive the menu.
Price display Prices listed per item No prices shown Step 07 direction A: premium fine dining sites globally do not display prices on the main landing page. Prices belong in a full menu accessed after the decision to visit is made. Removing them keeps the page focused on experience, not transaction.

The complete transformation · Before and After
Category average · Before
  • Generic headline claiming the "unforgettable experience"
  • Warm beige palette with no distinct identity
  • Category tags signalling confusion about positioning
  • PDF menu download breaking the browsing flow
  • Prices displayed — page reads as a transaction
  • No cooking method visible anywhere
  • No scarcity signal — availability appears infinite
  • "Book Now" CTA — urgent, generic, undistinctive
  • No mobile-first consideration in layout
Ember & Oak · After
  • Specific headline stating the cooking method and the brand
  • Near-black Noir palette with single ember-orange accent
  • Wood type strip replacing tags — method as identity
  • Native HTML menu — instant, readable, indexed
  • No prices — page reads as an experience invitation
  • Named wood per dish throughout every section
  • "28 guests each evening" at the first viewport
  • "Reserve a Table" — quiet, considered, brand-consistent
  • Mobile hamburger menu · responsive at every breakpoint
