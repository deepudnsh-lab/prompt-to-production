Maya is a Product Manager at a software company. She lives with her husband and their 4-year-old Golden Retriever, Biscuit. She spends approximately $380 per year on grooming products, treats, and wellness items for Biscuit — about 8% of her total household discretionary spending. She discovers products through Instagram (following dog grooming accounts, trainer recommendations, dog wellness creators), verifies ingredients through Reddit and Facebook dog groups, and makes purchase decisions based on ingredient transparency and other dog owners' experiences.

"I want to know exactly what's in the shampoo I'm putting on Biscuit's coat. If a brand won't tell me the percentages, I assume they have something to hide."

02 — How Maya shops

Six behaviors
that define purchase decisions.

  • B01 — Photographs before and after grooming Posts grooming photos to Instagram 2-3 times per week. Looks for visible coat improvements (shine, texture) as proof of product efficacy. Reads captions on other dog owners' grooming posts for product mentions.
  • B02 — Researches ingredients obsessively Before purchase, reads the full ingredient list and researches each unknown ingredient on Google. Looks for percentages to assess concentration. Distrusts brands that use vague terms like "natural botanical blend."
  • B03 — Asks the dog owner community first When considering a new product, posts in Austin dog groups asking "Has anyone tried [brand]?" Trusts peer recommendations over advertising. Wants to hear about actual results, not marketing claims.
  • B04 — Follows dog grooming creators Follows 12+ accounts focused on dog grooming and coat health. Watches tutorials on proper shampoo technique. Clicks links in creator bios when they recommend products. Follows recommendations from dog trainers and veterinarians on Instagram.
  • B05 — Commits to products that work Once a product proves effective on Biscuit's coat, becomes a loyal repeat customer. Does not shop around if current product is working. Stops researching. Builds routine.
  • B06 — Treats dog care spending as investment Does not compare to human product pricing. Views $40 shampoo as normal. Sees dog wellness as health spend, not discretionary spending. Willing to pay premium for ingredient transparency.
03 — Purchase journey

Five stages
to decision.

1
Discover — Sees a dog grooming post on Instagram. Creator mentions "Pawside shampoo changed Biscuit's coat." Clicks link. Arrives at landing page.
2
Research — Reads the full ingredient list. Sees percentages. Verifies each ingredient on Google. Checks the dog owner Reddit communities for mentions of the brand. Posts in Austin dog group: "Has anyone tried Pawside?"
3
Verify — Finds 3-4 positive comments from Austin dog owners who tried Pawside. No negative reviews. Reads testimonials from other dog owners on the landing page. Sees photos of actual dogs (not models) with healthy coats.
4
Purchase — Orders a single bottle ($28-$35 price point feels reasonable). Adds to cart. Checks out. Does not look for discount codes (willing to pay full price for transparency).
5
Commit — Product proves effective. Biscuit's coat improves. Posts before/after photos on Instagram mentioning Pawside. Repeats order every 3-4 weeks without researching alternatives. Becomes brand advocate.

The entire journey from discover to purchase takes 2-7 days. The commitment phase lasts 6+ months (or until product runs out). Maya is looking for a reason to trust — ingredient percentages and peer recommendations provide that reason.

04 — Design implications

What Maya needs
from Pawside.

Insight 01 — Ingredient percentages are trust signals
Not optional. Essential.
Maya will spend 15 minutes researching ingredients before purchase. If percentages are missing, she will assume they are hidden for a reason. Ingredient transparency is not a nice-to-have feature — it is the primary proof of quality.
Insight 02 — Dog owner testimonials work better than anything
Real dogs. Real people. Real results.
Maya distrusts professional photography and marketing copy. She trusts testimonials from other dog owners who have actually used the product. Photos of real dogs (with their actual owners) are more persuasive than any brand photography.
Insight 03 — The landing page is research territory
Make it easy to verify.
Maya will read every section of a landing page looking for proof points. Long-form copy, detailed ingredient information, and peer testimonials should be on the page itself — not hidden behind tabs or links. She is in research mode and wants everything visible.
Insight 04 — Instagram and dog communities drive decisions
Where Maya lives.
Landing page alone is not enough. Pawside needs to show up in dog owner conversations organically. This means dog grooming creators must recommend the product. The community must validate the brand. Maya will not buy based on advertising — she will buy based on peer recommendation.
