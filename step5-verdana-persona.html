01 — The ideal customer

Meet Sloane.
She buys the jar first.

Sloane is a 29-year-old UX designer at a tech company in Brooklyn. She earns well, lives alone, and treats her skincare routine as the most intentional 15 minutes of her day. Her bathroom shelf is curated the way her apartment is curated — every product earns its place by looking right and working right, in that order.

She discovered skincare as a self-care practice during the pandemic, graduated from drugstore to premium within two years, and now spends more on skincare than on clothing. She knows the difference between retinol and retinal. She knows what bakuchiol is and why it matters for a 29-year-old with sensitive skin. She follows three dermatologists on TikTok and two ingredient chemists on Instagram. She still buys the jar because of how it looks on her shelf.

The buyer thesis: Sloane does not choose between beautiful and effective. She requires both — and she will pay $150 for the brand that delivers both without making her feel like she is compromising either.

02 — Six defining behaviours

How Sloane
actually shops.

B01
She discovers on TikTok and Instagram, buys on the brand's website
Sloane finds new brands through short-form video — texture clips, founder stories, ingredient breakdowns by dermatologists she follows. But she does not buy through the app. She opens the brand website in Safari, reads the full product page, looks at every photo, and only then adds to cart. The brand website is where the sale is won or lost — not the app where she discovered it.
B02
She buys on aesthetics first, validates on ingredients second
The sequence is always the same: packaging stops the scroll → photography creates desire → ingredient list confirms the decision. She will not buy a product that does not look right on her shelf, no matter how strong the formula. She will not keep a product that looks right but contains silicones or fragrances she has decided to avoid. Both gates must be passed.
B03
She reads every ingredient at percentage level when she can find it
Sloane uses INCIDecoder and CosDNA to cross-reference formulas she cannot decode herself. She follows Caroline Hirons and Hyram on YouTube. She knows that "fragrance" in an INCI list is a red flag for sensitive skin. A brand that names its percentages — "5% bakuchiol, 2% squalane" — earns trust instantly. A brand that says "with bakuchiol" without naming the percentage triggers suspicion.
B04
She photographs every product she buys and posts the shelfie
Sloane's Instagram has 12,000 followers built around her apartment, her shelf, and her routine. Every new product she buys is photographed before it is used. The packaging is content before the formula is content. A brand she buys from will appear in posts seen by 12,000 people who share her aesthetic and her income bracket. The unboxing experience is not optional — it is the first distribution channel.
B05
She is deeply loyal to brands that have never disappointed her
Sloane's current routine has six products from four brands. Three of those brands she has repurchased without re-evaluating for over a year. Once a brand earns her trust — through formula consistency, packaging integrity, and communication honesty — she does not comparison-shop. She simply re-orders. The conversion cost is high. The retention cost is low.
B06
She treats premium skincare as an investment, not an indulgence
Sloane does not experience guilt at a $150 price point. She frames skincare as preventative healthcare — cheaper than Botox, more reliable than any wellness supplement, more lasting than a restaurant meal. She will reject a brand that apologises for its price — phrases like "affordable luxury" or "without the luxury markup" read as insecurity. She wants the brand to know it is worth what it costs.

04 — Trust signals vs friction points

What converts Sloane.
What loses her.

✓ Trust signals — what converts
Named percentages in the ingredient list — "5% niacinamide" not "with niacinamide." The specificity is the proof.
Warm, tactile photography — natural light, linen or stone surfaces, the product in use, texture visible. Not a white sweep studio shot.
Glass packaging — frosted or clear. Signals seriousness at the $80–$300 tier. Plastic at this price point is a credibility failure.
Founder who knows the formula — not a lifestyle founder, a formulation founder. Someone who can answer "why 5% and not 7%?" specifically.
Clean INCI list — no fragrance, no parabens, no silicones. Sloane can read the list and will. She will catch anything she does not want in her routine.
Editorial-quality brand visuals — the brand must look like it belongs in a magazine. If the photography looks like a Shopify template, the $150 price tag is dissonant.
✕ Friction points — what loses her
"Natural" without specifics — "made with natural ingredients" is the most common greenwashing phrase in the category. Sloane knows it and distrusts it immediately.
Fragrance listed as "fragrance" — this is a known irritant mask on INCI lists. Sloane will see it, flag it, and leave the product page without purchasing.
Plastic packaging at premium prices — any product priced above $60 in a plastic tube or jar fails Sloane's first visual credibility check. She does not read past the packaging.
Vague efficacy claims — "visibly transforms skin" means nothing without a timeframe, a percentage, or a mechanism. Sloane wants to know what it does and why.
Forced account creation before purchase — any checkout gate that requires an account before buy will lose Sloane at the critical moment. Guest checkout is non-negotiable.
Apologising for the price — "affordable luxury," "without the markup," "accessible high-performance." Sloane interprets these as a brand that does not believe in its own value. It makes her believe it less too.
05 — Six design implications for Verdana

What Sloane means
for the brand.

D01
Packaging photography must be the hero — not a lifestyle afterthought
The product in natural light, on a warm linen or stone surface, with texture visible, is the single most important image on the website. Not a model. Not a lifestyle scene. The jar itself, photographed with the care of fine jewellery. Sloane's first gate is visual — if the photography does not stop her scroll, the formula never gets a chance.
D02
Ingredient transparency must be structural — not a footnote
The product page must lead with ingredient names and percentages — not bury them at the bottom in small type. "5% bakuchiol sourced from Psoralea corylifolia, cold-pressed. 2% squalane from sugarcane, not shark liver." This is not marketing copy — it is the proof that the formula deserves the price. It must be the most prominent information on the page after the product name.
D03
Copy must not apologise for price or explain luxury
No phrases like "accessible luxury," "affordable high-performance," or "without the department store markup." Verdana's price is stated plainly — $128 — with no justification and no discount framing. The brand knows what it costs and why. A buyer who needs to be convinced it is worth it is not Sloane. A buyer who needs the price confirmed is.
D04
The visual system must be shelfie-ready
Sloane will photograph Verdana before she uses it. The brand palette — cream, sage, warm terracotta — must look beautiful against the warm tones of a Brooklyn apartment, against linen, against marble. The product must be designed for the camera. The label typography must be legible in an Instagram photo at 50% zoom. This is not vanity — it is distribution.
D05
The editorial register must match a $150 price point
Cormorant Garamond at display scale, generous line height, warm cream surface — the visual system must communicate the same category of luxury as the product it describes. If the website looks like a Squarespace template, the $150 jar becomes dissonant. The design is not decoration — it is the brand's first proof that it belongs at this tier.
D06
The unboxing experience must be designed for documentation
The packaging, tissue, insert card, and outer box must be photographable — warm colours, premium materials, thoughtful detail. An insert card with the product's key ingredients written in the brand typeface. A note that feels personal. The unboxing post is Verdana's most efficient marketing channel — Sloane's 12,000 followers are her exact buyer. The unboxing must earn that post.
