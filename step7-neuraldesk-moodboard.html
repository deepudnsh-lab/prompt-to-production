01 — Three directions

Three genuinely different
visual registers.

Each direction answers the same brief — a self-serve AI analytics product for Marcus Webb, pure red accent, white dominant — from a completely different emotional and typographic starting point. Direction A is the safest and most credible for the B2B context. Direction B is the most distinctive and human. Direction C is the most minimal and modern.

The direction name is the designer's internal reference. The vibe word is what the moodboard communicates emotionally. One describes the concept. The other describes the feeling.

What all three share: #FFFFFF white as the dominant colour · #E8000D pure red as the sole accent · Space Grotesk for headlines · IBM Plex Mono for data labels and metadata · pricing visible on the landing page · self-serve primary CTA. The directions differ in secondary palette, typography for body copy, imagery style, and emotional register.

Feeling
Bloomberg Terminal reimagined for non-technical users. Precise, authoritative, deliberately minimal. Red appears only where it must — CTAs, data highlights, the brand mark. White space is not absence — it is the design. The monospace label layer adds technical depth without adding complexity. Every element earns its place.
Who it appeals to
Marcus Webb exactly — a PM who evaluates software like a professional and wants a tool that looks as serious as the data it analyses. Appeals to startup founders, growth leads, and ops managers who share outputs in executive meetings and need those outputs to look credible. Does not appeal to buyers who want warmth or approachability first.
The risk
Can read as cold if executed without enough typographic warmth. The monochrome + single red palette requires strong hierarchy discipline — any layout sloppiness reads as unfinished, not minimal. Risk: a page that looks like a financial terminal and intimidates the non-technical buyer it was designed to attract.

Feeling
A fast-moving business publication crossed with a fintech product. Human, warm, urgent — but still precise. The editorial serif introduces personality without sacrificing credibility. The warm cream surface takes the chill off white without losing cleanliness. Red and orange together read as energy, not alarm. It feels like a tool built by people who use it themselves.
Who it appeals to
Founders and PMs who want software that feels like it was made by humans, not engineers. The startup and scale-up buyer who reads TechCrunch and shares newsletter recommendations. Appeals to growth and marketing buyers who value brand personality as a trust signal. Less appealing to enterprise-adjacent buyers who interpret warmth as less serious.
The risk
The serif display font is the highest-risk choice — editorial fonts in SaaS can read as a blog rather than a product. Requires strong UI component design to balance the softness of the typeface. The warm orange secondary accent needs to be used extremely sparingly or it will conflict with the red primary and muddy the palette.

Feeling
Linear or Vercel — maximum white space, minimum noise, total clarity. The light-weight display font creates a calm, unhurried register that makes complexity feel manageable. Cool blue-grey secondary palette introduces depth without warmth. Red appears almost sparingly — so scarce that each instance carries full visual weight. It is the most modern-feeling direction.
Who it appeals to
The design-literate PM — someone who uses Linear for issue tracking, Vercel for deploys, and Raycast for everything else. Buyers who interpret extreme minimalism as extreme quality. Developers-turned-PMs and technical founders who are comfortable with white space and distrust brands that over-explain themselves. Smaller audience than A but higher intent.
The risk
The lightest-weight font and least colour means the design has almost no safety net — layout and spacing carry everything. In the wrong hands, Direction C looks like an empty page. The cool blue-grey palette may feel too cold for a product that needs to communicate warmth and approachability to non-technical buyers. Niche appeal is its strength and its ceiling.

05 — Selecting a direction

Which direction
wins the brief.

Generate all three moodboards on Midjourney using the prompts above. Upload the images to the drop zones — they will embed automatically. Then choose a direction and confirm below. The selected direction becomes the locked visual system for Steps 8–13.

The recommendation — if the choice is difficult
Direction A (Precision/Signal) is the safest bet for Marcus Webb. It matches the visual register of the tools he already respects — Linear, Vercel, Notion — while the pure red accent gives NeuralDesk a distinctiveness none of those tools has. Direction B introduces warmth that the research does not specifically call for. Direction C is the highest-quality direction but the narrowest in its audience appeal. For a self-serve product that needs to convert broadly, A has the best risk-reward ratio. That said — this is your decision, not mine.
