01 — Audit methodology

Three sites. Five dimensions.
One gap.

Three direct competitors were audited through Sloane Mitchell's eyes — a 29-year-old UX designer discovering a new skincare brand on a Sunday morning with her second coffee. Each site is scored across five dimensions on a 1–5 scale. The scores are not about what the product does — they are about what each homepage communicates to Sloane in the first 30 seconds.

D
Design quality
I
Ingredient transparency
A
Aesthetic coherence
S
Storytelling clarity
W
Warmth & botanicals

Annotation system: Green annotation = something that works well for Sloane. Amber = an observation worth noting. Red = a design gap or weakness. Upload annotated screenshots to the drop zones below — prompts for each site are at the end of this document.

02 — Competitor 01

Tatcha.
The ceremony benchmark.

Tatcha
tatcha.com · Japanese-inspired luxury · $68–$185 · Sephora + DTC
D: 4/5 I: 1/5 A: 4/5 S: 1/5 W: 5/5
15/25
Total score
What works
The highest design quality in the category — the deep lacquer red, gold accents, and Japanese heritage photography are immediately distinctive and unmistakably premium. Tatcha stops Sloane's scroll. The product photography is editorial-quality: natural light, warm surfaces, textures visible. The brand story (founder Victoria Tsai discovering geisha skincare rituals in Kyoto) is specific, credible, and emotionally resonant. Sephora placement adds instant credibility for a new visitor.
Where it falls short
Ingredient transparency is where Tatcha loses the Sloane buyer. The product pages lead with ritual and heritage — the ingredient communication is vague, using ingredient names as evocative markers rather than clinical specifics. "Hadasei-3™ complex" is branded, not transparent. Sloane cannot find percentages. The INCIDecoder community finds Tatcha formulas less clean than the brand positioning suggests. And critically: the palette is lacquer red and gold — beautiful, but cold and Japanese, not warm and botanical.
What Verdana takes
The editorial photography standard — warmth, texture, natural light, product as the hero. The commitment to premium design at every touchpoint. The specificity of the founder story. What Verdana does not take: the proprietary ingredient language, the cold lacquer palette, the opacity around formula specifics. Verdana takes Tatcha's design register and applies it to botanical transparency instead of ritual ceremony.
Design gap
Tatcha has the ceremony, the design quality, and the premium positioning — but not the ingredient transparency that Sloane's generation now requires as a baseline. A buyer who takes Tatcha's photography and applies it to an honest, named-percentage formula is a brand Tatcha has not built. That is Verdana.
03 — Competitor 02

Drunk Elephant.
Transparent but not elevated.

Drunk Elephant
drunkelephant.com · Ingredient-led · $28–$90 · Sephora hero brand
D: 3/5 I: 2/5 A: 5/5 S: 2/5 W: 1/5
13/25
Total score
What works
The strongest ingredient communication in the category — the "Suspicious 6" framework (the six ingredients Drunk Elephant avoids) is the most memorable clean beauty communication device in the market. Sloane knows what it is before she visits the site. The transparency is genuine and verifiable. The brand's story (founder Tiffany Masterson's sensitive skin journey) is specific and credible. Sephora hero placement means every millennial has encountered it.
Where it falls short
The design is playful, colourful, and fun — and completely wrong for a $150 price point. Drunk Elephant's packaging looks like a toy store, not a luxury skincare brand. The neon palette (hot pink, acid yellow, electric blue) is distinctive but not elevated. Sloane will not place Drunk Elephant on the same shelf as Tatcha — it does not look right. Acquired by Shiseido in 2019, some community members feel the soul has shifted. Priced below Verdana's target tier.
What Verdana takes
The ingredient transparency model — name the ingredients, name what you avoid, name why. The "Suspicious 6" is a communications framework as much as a formula decision. Verdana builds the same transparency architecture at a higher design register. Where Drunk Elephant uses neon and playfulness to signal transparency, Verdana uses warm editorial typography and botanical naming to signal the same honesty — at a visual quality that justifies $120–$180.
Design gap
Drunk Elephant has the transparency Sloane trusts and the community she belongs to — but the visual design does not match the premium price point she is ready to inhabit. A brand with Drunk Elephant's ingredient honesty and Tatcha's visual register does not exist yet. Verdana is that brand.
04 — Competitor 03

Augustinus Bader.
Authority without warmth.

Augustinus Bader
augustinusbader.com · Clinical luxury · $80–$320 · DTC + luxury retail
D: 3.5/5 I: 3/5 A: 3/5 S: 2.5/5 W: 2/5
14/25
Total score
What works
The strongest founder story in the category — Professor Augustinus Bader's decades of stem cell research at the University of Leipzig is an extraordinary credential that no lifestyle founder can manufacture. The science is real and the storytelling is unflinching. The white and gold clinical aesthetic communicates authority. Celebrity following (Hailey Bieber, Victoria Beckham) provides social proof at the highest cultural tier. Priced at the top of Verdana's target range — proves the market exists.
Where it falls short
The aesthetic coherence is the lowest of the three competitors — the clinical white packaging communicates authority but not warmth, ritual, or desire. Sloane will not photograph this on her linen shelf. The unboxing is functional, not beautiful. The homepage is text-heavy and cold — it reads like a pharmaceutical paper, not a skincare experience. The warmth score is 1/5 because warmth is deliberately absent — it is a feature for their buyer, a dealbreaker for Sloane's.
What Verdana takes
The confidence to lead with science without apology. The founder-as-credentialed-expert story structure. The refusal to reduce the brand to a lifestyle aesthetic at the expense of the formula story. What Verdana does not take: the cold clinical palette, the pharmaceutical visual register, the design that does not invite photography. Verdana takes Augustinus Bader's intellectual authority and places it inside a warm, photographable, botanically-rooted visual world.
Design gap
Augustinus Bader proves that serious science at premium prices has a large, willing audience — but it has not attempted to make that science warm, beautiful, or photographable. The buyer who wants the credibility of Augustinus Bader and the aesthetics of Tatcha has nowhere to go. Verdana is built specifically for that buyer.

07 — Design opportunity statement

What the audit
confirms.

The three-site audit confirms the finding from Step 4: the US premium clean beauty category has a visible, unoccupied position at the intersection of warm botanical visual identity, named ingredient transparency, and luxury tier pricing. Every competitor has two of the three. None has all three simultaneously.

The audit makes one additional finding that the research alone could not: the warmth gap is visual, not just conceptual. Every competitor homepage is either cold (Augustinus Bader, clinical white), bright and playful (Drunk Elephant, neon palette), or heritage-cold (Tatcha, lacquer red). Not one of them has a warm cream-and-sage palette that would look beautiful photographed in natural light on a linen surface in a Brooklyn apartment. That specific visual gap — the shelfie-ready warmth gap — is Verdana's territory.

Design opportunity · Verdana · confirmed by audit
Tatcha builds for the ceremony. Drunk Elephant builds for the formula. Augustinus Bader builds for the science. Verdana builds for Sloane — the buyer who needs the ceremony, trusts the formula, respects the science, and photographs the jar before she opens it.
