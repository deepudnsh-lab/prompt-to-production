01 — Why selection matters

A portfolio that covers
six random industries
proves nothing.

The industries chosen for this project needed to satisfy a specific set of conditions — not personal preference, and not what happens to look impressive in a thumbnail. They needed to collectively demonstrate that the AI-directed design workflow can produce meaningfully different visual results across meaningfully different contexts.

If two chosen industries share the same audience, emotional tone, and visual language, they're redundant in a portfolio. The goal was to find six industries that sit as far apart from each other as possible on every design dimension — so each case study teaches the viewer something the others don't.

16 industries were considered. Each was scored against four criteria. The six highest scorers — with the most distinct combination of scores — were selected. The process is documented below exactly as it happened.

02 — The four scoring criteria

What we scored
against — and why.

Each criterion was chosen because it reflects a real skill a design recruiter or creative director looks for. Together, the four criteria measure whether a case study demonstrates design range, audience understanding, technical challenge, and real-world applicability.

A
Visual diversity
How distinct is this industry's visual language from the others in the set? Does it bring a different palette, typographic voice, layout density, and aesthetic direction — or does it duplicate territory already covered?
Score 1–5
B
Persona clarity
How sharply defined is the person this website is speaking to? A well-defined persona produces sharper copy, more considered UX decisions, and a more defensible case study. Broad or undefined audiences score lower.
Score 1–5
C
Design challenge
How much does this industry push the designer's craft? Does it require solving real layout problems, navigating a complex content hierarchy, or handling a nuanced brand tone that AI alone cannot resolve without strategic direction?
Score 1–5
D
Emotional range
How wide and distinct is the emotional register this industry asks the designer to work in? Does it require a feeling — warmth, luxury, excitement, trust, wonder, playfulness — that none of the other selected industries already occupy?
Score 1–5

On scoring: Scores reflect how well each industry serves the portfolio's overall purpose — demonstrating range across a directed AI workflow. A score of 3 on visual distinctiveness doesn't mean the industry is uninteresting; it means another selected industry already occupies that visual territory and adding this one would be redundant.

04 — Selection rationale · the chosen six

Why each of the six
earned its place.

Scoring alone doesn't tell the full story. The six selected industries also needed to work as a set — covering the widest possible spread of visual tone, audience type, and content structure between them.

01
Fine dining · hospitality
Fine dining / hospitality
Scored highest overall. Fine dining demands a sophisticated, editorial visual language — dark palettes, cinematic imagery, restrained typography, and a clear conversion goal (reservation). It sets a high bar at the start of the series and tests whether AI can produce a genuinely premium feel without generic stock aesthetics.
Visual 5/5
Audience 5/5
Complexity 4/5
Emotional 5/5
02
Space tourism · aerospace
Space tourism / aerospace
The most visually extreme case in the set. Space tourism requires a cinematic, full-bleed dark UI with dramatic type and a sense of scale that few other industries demand. It sits at the opposite end of the visual spectrum from the fine dining brand and tests the AI's ability to produce futuristic, high-stakes design language.
Visual 5/5
Audience 4/5
Complexity 5/5
Emotional 4/5
03
Kids entertainment · EdTech
Kids entertainment / EdTech
The most visually distinct from every other case in the set. Kids brands use rounded forms, bright saturated palettes, playful typography, and a dual audience (children as users, parents as decision-makers) that requires two separate layers of communication on one page. No other industry in the list demands this kind of tonal shift.
Visual 5/5
Audience 5/5
Complexity 4/5
Emotional 4/5
04
AI SaaS · B2B tech
AI SaaS / B2B tech
The most commercially relevant case for a design lead seeking work with tech companies. B2B SaaS landing pages have specific structural conventions — feature grids, social proof, pricing tables, and a CTA architecture — that require design systems thinking, not just aesthetic judgement. It also directly reflects the industry context of Deepu's last two roles.
Visual 4/5
Audience 5/5
Complexity 5/5
Emotional 5/5
05
Luxury skincare · beauty
Luxury skincare / beauty
Beauty and skincare have one of the most refined and well-established visual languages in consumer design — extreme whitespace, muted palettes, editorial photography, and a tone of quiet authority. It tests the AI's ability to produce restraint rather than decoration, and sits in deliberate contrast to the expressive energy of the space tourism and kids entertainment brands.
Visual 4/5
Audience 5/5
Complexity 4/5
Emotional 5/5
06
Pet care · consumer D2C
Pet care / consumer D2C
Pet care closes the set with warmth and emotional accessibility — the tonal opposite of the AI SaaS and space tourism brands. Consumer D2C brands in this space rely on trust, community, and visual friendliness. It also has a clearly defined, data-supported audience (urban pet owners, typically 25–40) which makes the persona exercise highly grounded and defensible.
Visual 4/5
Audience 5/5
Complexity 3/5
Emotional 5/5
05 — The ten that didn't make the cut

Why they were
set aside.

None of the ten industries below are weak design subjects. Each was set aside for a specific, portfolio-level reason — either because another selected industry already covers similar visual territory, or because it didn't add enough range to the overall set.

Fintech / neobanking
Overlaps with the selected AI SaaS entry in visual language and audience type — both are dark-mode, trust-led, tech-facing interfaces. Also scores low on emotional range: financial services design is deliberately unemotional, and that register is already covered by the B2B tech selection.
Architecture / real estate
Shares the restrained, aspirational editorial tone of the selected luxury skincare brand. Both industries use whitespace, muted palettes, and understated typography — adding architecture would duplicate visual territory without expanding emotional range.
Music streaming
Audience definition is too broad — music streaming speaks to everyone, making persona work less precise. Shares the bold, expressive emotional register of the selected space tourism brand (excitement, energy, escapism) without adding a new emotional dimension to the set.
Gaming / esports
Visually very strong and emotionally rich. Set aside because persona clarity is lower — the gaming audience is broad and fragmented, making it harder to build a precise, defensible ICP. The emotional register (excitement, competition) is also partially covered by the space tourism entry.
Language learning
Sits between two selected industries without matching either's distinctiveness. Visually it shares brightness with the kids entertainment brand; structurally it resembles the AI SaaS entry. The emotional register — gentle encouragement, progress — doesn't add a new dimension to the set.
Health & fitness / gym
The emotional register — motivation, ambition, physical transformation — partially overlaps with the space tourism brand's sense of ambition and scale. Visually, the bold type and high-contrast aesthetic is also covered. Doesn't expand the emotional map of the portfolio.
HealthTech / women's health
The emotional register — reassurance, clinical trust, vulnerability — is distinct and interesting. Set aside because the visual language overlaps significantly with the luxury skincare brand's soft, trust-led palette, and the content complexity (medical sensitivity, regulatory tone) goes beyond what a single landing page case study can demonstrate well.
Art supply / D2C retail
Playful and colourful — emotional territory already covered more distinctly by the kids entertainment brand. The adult creative audience is harder to pin down precisely than the kids entertainment brand's dual audience (child + parent), weakening the persona exercise.
Boutique hotel / travel
Shares both the visual language and the emotional register of the selected fine dining brand — warmth, sensory indulgence, aspirational escapism. Two hospitality-adjacent brands would look like a repeat, not a demonstration of range.
Professional e-learning
Scored lowest overall. Shares both the visual structure of the AI SaaS entry and the low emotional range of functional B2B design. Professional e-learning pages solve a real problem but rarely ask for a distinctive visual or emotional register — making them a weak case study subject.
