01 — The ideal customer

Meet Arjun.
He has already done everything else.

OrbitX's customer is not a thrill-seeker. He is not buying a rollercoaster. He is a person who has built something significant in his life — a company, a body of work, a legacy — and who is now looking for the one experience that cannot be replicated, purchased again, or upgraded. Space is not on his bucket list. It is the last thing on it.

Arjun founded a logistics technology company in Mumbai in 1999. He sold a majority stake in 2018 and now chairs three portfolio companies across India, the UAE, and Singapore. He flies business class on autopilot. He has stayed in every six-star hotel on the list. He climbed Kilimanjaro in 2019. He drove a Formula 3 car at Silverstone in 2022. Each of these was a version of the same question: what does it feel like to be at the edge of something? Space is the final answer to that question.

He is not reckless. He spent six months researching the safety record of New Shepard before making any inquiries. He asked his cardiologist about the physiological effects of launch G-forces before he told his wife he was considering it. He will read OrbitX's vehicle specifications before he reads the experience description. He needs to trust the engineering before he allows himself to want the experience.

03 — Six defining behaviours

How Arjun
decides.

Behaviour 01
He researches before he expresses interest
Arjun will have read everything publicly available about OrbitX before his first contact. He will know the vehicle's safety record, the training duration, the altitude reached, and the names of previous passengers. The first time he interacts with the site he already knows whether he wants to go. He is looking for confirmation, not education.
Behaviour 02
He moves through the decision with one trusted advisor
Not a committee. One person — his EA, or a long-term colleague — who manages the logistics. He will not fill in a form. He will not send an email to a generic address. If the OrbitX website does not offer a clearly human point of contact — a name, a title, a direct line — he will stop. He is accustomed to speaking to a person who is expecting his call.
Behaviour 03
He views scarcity as a quality signal, not a pressure tactic
Being told there are 6 available seats does not make Arjun feel rushed. It makes him feel he is in the right place. Scarcity in his world means quality — his own business had waiting lists at its peak. He interprets limited availability as a signal that OrbitX controls the experience rather than scaling it. He does not want to be on a flight with 40 people.
Behaviour 04
He will tell the story more than he will relive the experience
The flight is 11 minutes. The story lasts a lifetime. Arjun is acutely aware that going to space will define the dinner party version of himself for the next decade. He is partly buying the experience and partly buying the narrative that follows it. OrbitX's design must give him the language to describe what he is about to do — before, during, and after.
Behaviour 05
He reads the page on a 14" laptop at 11pm in a hotel room
Not on a phone. Not in an office. He opens OrbitX's website in a rare quiet moment — on a work trip, after dinner, when the schedule has briefly cleared. He has 20 minutes. The page must earn his time in the first 60 seconds or he closes the tab and adds it to a list he will return to — which he sometimes does and sometimes does not.
Behaviour 06
He does not want to be sold to
Any language that feels like marketing — superlatives, urgency copy, "book now" buttons, countdown timers — will register as a mismatch. He is accustomed to being the one people want something from. The OrbitX website must feel like it is informing him of an opportunity, not persuading him toward a purchase. The tone of a briefing, not a brochure.

06 — Design implications

What Arjun
demands from the design.

01
Technical credibility must appear before the experience narrative
The vehicle name, flight history, altitude, and safety certifications belong in the hero or immediately below it — not in a dedicated "Safety" page buried in the nav. Arjun will not navigate there. If he cannot see the credentials immediately, he will assume they are not strong enough to display prominently.
02
The CTA must never say "Book Now" or "Buy a Ticket"
"Speak to a mission specialist" is correct. "Reserve your manifest position" is correct. "Join the waitlist" is acceptable. Any language that positions Arjun as a customer buying a product will register as a mismatch. He is a mission candidate being considered for a programme. The framing must honour that.
03
Previous passengers are the most powerful social proof available
Arjun will look for people like himself — founders, chairs, accomplished individuals — who have already done this. Named passengers with brief biographical context are more convincing than testimonials. "Passenger 34 — Founder, fintech sector, Mumbai, flew October 2026" tells him everything he needs to know: someone in his peer group went, and they trusted it.
04
The training programme is a feature, not a disclaimer
Two days of pre-flight training at the spaceport is not a barrier — for Arjun it is part of the experience. The Formula 3 day at Silverstone had a briefing and a technical debrief. That was part of what made it worthwhile. OrbitX's design should present the training schedule as a premium itinerary, not a requirement to be minimised.
05
Give him the language before the experience
Arjun will describe this to people for years. The copy on OrbitX's page will become his vocabulary. "At 100km above the Earth, the atmosphere disappears. What you see is not a view — it is the actual planet, suspended in actual darkness." That line will be how he describes it at dinner. The copy is not just selling the experience. It is giving him the words to own it.
06
The visual language must feel aerospace, not adventure tourism
Adventure tourism design — bright colours, action photography, bold sans-serif CTAs — is for a different customer. Arjun is not buying an extreme sport. He is joining a programme. The visual register should reference mission control, aerospace engineering, and precision manufacturing — not skydiving or mountain expeditions. Dark, precise, and vast. Not loud.
