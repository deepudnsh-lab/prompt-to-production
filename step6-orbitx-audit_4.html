01 — What this audit covers

Three sites.
The same gap.

The three most visible space tourism websites — Blue Origin, Virgin Galactic, and SpaceX — all operate in the same visual territory: dark backgrounds, Earth-from-space photography, technical credibility signals, and a CTA that leads toward a waitlist or contact form. This audit examines each site against five criteria and looks for the gap that all three share — the design territory that OrbitX can claim.

All observations are based on publicly documented design patterns observed on each site as of early 2026. Drop zones below are for annotated screenshots to be captured using the AI browser tool prompts in the Step 06 Supplement document.

The universal gap: None of the three sites puts the human traveller at the centre of the hero. All three lead with either the vehicle or the view. The person who went — their identity, their motivation, their transformation — is absent from every first viewport in this category. That gap is OrbitX's design territory.

What it does well
  • Visual impact 5/5 — cinematic full-viewport launch photography. The New Shepard rocket against deep blue sky is one of the strongest hero images in the commercial space category. Immediate authority.
  • Navigation is clean and functional. "New Shepard," "New Glenn," "Blue Moon" — product categories are immediately legible. No confusion about what Blue Origin does.
  • Mission statement — "We go to space to benefit Earth" — is brief, memorable, and philosophically distinct from competitors. Six words that establish a point of view.
  • Mobile quality 4/5 — the hero scales well. The rocket image retains impact at smaller viewports. Text remains legible without requiring zoom.
Where the design falls short
  • Philosophy clarity 3/5 — "We go to space to benefit Earth" is a mission statement, not a product proposition. It does not tell Arjun what Blue Origin will do for him, or why he should choose it over Virgin Galactic.
  • The passenger is invisible. Fifty-eight people have flown on New Shepard. None of them appear on the homepage. Their stories, identities, and motivations are entirely absent from the hero and the primary navigation.
  • Reservation path is unclear. There is no prominent CTA for civilian passengers. The path from "I want to go" to "here is how I begin" requires multiple clicks and significant effort.
  • Warmth 3/5 — the palette is cold blue-black. Technically credible but emotionally distant. The site communicates aerospace engineering confidence without communicating human invitation.
What OrbitX takes
  • The launch photography format works — vehicle against sky, full viewport, maximum visual authority. OrbitX will adopt this as a starting point and add the human element that Blue Origin omits.
  • Six-word mission statement structure — "We go to space to benefit Earth" — is a strong model. OrbitX needs an equivalent statement that is equally brief and more passenger-centred.
  • The passenger absence is OrbitX's largest opportunity. What Blue Origin treats as a privacy decision, OrbitX will treat as a design decision — named passengers, brief biographies, the human at the centre of the hero.

What it does well
  • Philosophy clarity 4/5 — "Space for All Who Dream" is the clearest passenger-centred mission statement in the category. It positions the aspiration rather than the vehicle, which is closer to OrbitX's territory.
  • Warmth 4/5 — softer whites and silver-grey tones create a more approachable brand aesthetic than Blue Origin's cold blue-black. The Virgin Galactic palette feels like luxury aviation rather than pure aerospace.
  • The spaceplane format — runway takeoff, elegant glide — communicates a more familiar and less intimidating journey than a vertical rocket launch. Design reinforces this with smoother, more organic photography.
  • Experience language appears earlier in the hierarchy than competitor sites. References to weightlessness, the view, and the transformation of the experience are present in the hero or near-hero sections.
Where the design falls short
  • Reservation clarity 3/5 — with 700+ people on the waitlist and a $600,000 ticket price, the conversion path is surprisingly unclear. The primary CTA leads to a general enquiry rather than a structured manifest reservation process.
  • Mobile quality 3/5 — the site does not perform as strongly on mobile as Blue Origin. The visual hierarchy loses its impact at smaller viewports. Some sections require horizontal scrolling on narrow screens.
  • The passenger is still absent. Despite being the most passenger-forward brand in the category, named travellers do not appear in the hero or primary sections. The transformation narrative is described rather than embodied.
  • The Delta-class development timeline is not clearly communicated. A $600,000 commitment to a product that is still in development requires clear progress signals — which are not prominent at hero level.
What OrbitX takes
  • "Space for All Who Dream" is the best mission statement in the category — but it is aspirational rather than specific. OrbitX's equivalent will be specific: who goes, what they experience, what returns with them.
  • The warmer palette approach is directionally correct — OrbitX will carry this further, introducing elements of human warmth (skin tones, interior lighting, the face of a passenger looking out) into a category dominated by cold spacecraft exteriors.
  • Waitlist scarcity signal — 700+ people — should be visible in the hero. Virgin Galactic does not display this. OrbitX will. "Current manifest: 14 of 24 seats reserved for Q3 2027" is more compelling than any superlative.

What it does well
  • Visual impact 5/5 — SpaceX's site is the most visually radical in the category. Minimal interface, enormous imagery, extreme restraint. The design communicates engineering confidence at a level no competitor matches.
  • Philosophy clarity 5/5 — "Making Life Multiplanetary" is the boldest mission statement in any industry. It does not describe what SpaceX does — it describes what SpaceX believes. It requires no explanation and inspires immediate respect.
  • The visual language of mission briefings — monochrome, technical, precise — is executed with total conviction. SpaceX's design does not try to appeal to everyone. It is built for a specific kind of person, and that specificity is its strength.
  • Mobile 4/5 — the minimal design scales extremely well. Less content to manage at smaller viewports means the site rarely feels cramped or truncated on mobile.
Where the design falls short
  • Warmth 2/5 — SpaceX's design is the coldest in the category. It communicates engineering ambition, not human invitation. Arjun would respect it immediately and feel unwelcome in it immediately. These are not incompatible responses.
  • Reservation clarity 2/5 — there is effectively no consumer conversion path on spacex.com. The site does not solicit civilian passengers. If you want to go to space on SpaceX, you find out through other channels. Not a gap for SpaceX — a gap for the category.
  • The human is completely absent. SpaceX's site does not discuss what it is like to be a passenger. The Inspiration4 mission — three days in orbit with four civilians — barely appears in the primary navigation. The engineering is everything; the traveller is a footnote.
What OrbitX takes
  • Radical restraint as a luxury signal — SpaceX proves that removing elements communicates confidence. OrbitX will adopt this principle: the page does not explain itself. It presents itself.
  • The mission statement format — three words, no punctuation, absolute conviction — is a benchmark OrbitX should aspire to. Not a description. A belief.
  • OrbitX occupies the space between Virgin Galactic's warmth and SpaceX's precision. Technically serious, humanly warm. The SpaceX audit defines one edge of that corridor; Virgin Galactic defines the other. OrbitX sits between them — closer to SpaceX on engineering credibility, closer to Virgin Galactic on human invitation.
03 — Universal gap analysis

What none of them
do.

The three sites represent three different aesthetic and strategic positions within space tourism. But they share four design gaps — things that no competitor does, and that OrbitX can make its own.

Design gapBlue OriginVirgin GalacticSpaceXOrbitX opportunity
The passenger is the hero Missing Missing Missing Named previous passengers with brief biographies in the primary hero section. Not testimonials — biographical signals. "Passenger 14 — Founder, Mumbai, flew April 2027."
Specific scarcity signal Missing Missing Missing "Current manifest: 14 of 24 seats reserved · Q3 2027 · 10 remaining." Real numbers, specific mission, visible at hero level. Not manufactured urgency — operational fact.
The training is a feature Buried Buried Missing Two days at the spaceport as a premium itinerary — simulator time, mission briefings, medical check, overnight stay — presented as part of the experience rather than a prerequisite to it.
Human-scale mission language Missing Partial Missing "At 100km, the atmosphere disappears. What remains is the planet — and you." Copy that gives the passenger the vocabulary to own what they are about to do. Sensory, specific, first-person in register.
Design opportunity statement · OrbitX
Blue Origin builds the rocket. Virgin Galactic builds the experience. SpaceX builds the future. OrbitX builds the passenger. Every competitor centres the vehicle or the mission. OrbitX centres the person who goes — who they are before, what they see during, and who they return as.
