02 — Nine decisions · category average vs OrbitX

What changed.
Why it changed.

Category average · Blue Origin · Virgin Galactic · SpaceX
OrbitX · Direction A · Cosmonaut / Vast
01
Hero alignment · the first structural signal
Left-aligned headline and body copy. Primary CTA bottom-left. Image fills right half of viewport.
Left alignment is the default for web design. Every competitor uses it. It communicates competence and familiarity — the visual language of a professional website. For space tourism, it reads as: hospitality, e-commerce, SaaS. It does not read as aerospace.
Centre-aligned headline, sub-copy, and CTAs. Full-viewport video behind everything. Text floats over the footage.
Centre alignment reads as aerospace. Mission posters, launch screens, pre-flight displays — all centred. The alignment alone signals that this is not a product page. It is a mission briefing. This was the single most impactful structural change to the landing page — one alignment decision changed the entire register.
02
Hero visual · what occupies the first viewport
Static photography of the vehicle. Rocket on pad, or rocket in flight. No person visible in the hero.
All three competitors lead with the vehicle. The rocket is the hero. This communicates engineering capability — which is the right signal for an engineering company. For a passenger experience company, it is the wrong signal. It answers "what does the rocket look like?" not "what does it feel like to go?"
Full-viewport AI-generated video. Earth from orbit. Stars. Atmosphere. Headline text centred over footage.
The view from space — not the vehicle that gets you there. The competitor audit found that no competitor shows the experience from the passenger's perspective at hero level. OrbitX leads with the view: what you will actually see. The vehicle is in The Vehicle section. The hero is about the destination.
03
Hero headline · the first words read
"Making Life Multiplanetary" — SpaceX "Space for All Who Dream" — Virgin Galactic "New Glenn · Orbital Rocket" — Blue Origin
Three different approaches — mission statement, aspiration, and product name. All three are outward-looking: about the company, the vehicle, or the vision. None of them place the passenger as the grammatical subject. None of them describe what the passenger will actually experience.
"At 100km, the atmosphere disappears."
A fact. Not a promise. Not a tagline. A description of what happens. The number grounds it. The consequence — the atmosphere disappears — is stated without embellishment. Arjun reads this and thinks: yes. That is what happens. The emotion arrives from the precision. This headline was not changed from the first draft of Step 8.
04
Passenger visibility · who appears on the page
No named civilian passengers on homepage. Blue Origin: 58 passengers flown. None appear by name or biography.
The passenger is invisible across all three competitor sites. Despite Blue Origin having flown 58 people and Virgin Galactic having 700+ on a waitlist, no named civilian passenger appears on any homepage. This was the single most significant gap identified in the Step 6 audit.
Passenger 08 · NX-03 Founder & Chairman · Manufacturing Mumbai · Flew March 2027
Biographical signal cards — not testimonials. Three previous passengers appear by role, sector, geography, and mission number. Identities kept confidential at request — which is itself a quality signal. Arjun sees his peer group: founder, Mumbai. He does not need a quote. He needs a mirror. The anonymity communicates that OrbitX protects its passengers.
05
Scarcity signal · how limited availability is communicated
"Limited availability" "Join the waitlist" "Reserve your seat" (generic)
No competitor displays actual mission designations, specific seat counts, or real launch windows at a visible level. The scarcity is implied but never stated. Arjun has spent his career distinguishing between genuine scarcity and manufactured urgency. Generic waitlist language reads as the second one.
NX-12 · Q1 2028 · 2 of 6 seats remaining NX-13 · Q2 2028 · 5 of 6 seats remaining NX-14 · Q3 2028 · 6 of 6 seats available
Real mission designations. Specific dates. Actual seat counts. Not manufactured urgency — operational fact. Three missions listed so the pipeline is visible. Arjun can choose his window. "2 of 6 seats remaining" is more compelling than "Limited availability" because it is true and specific. No exclamation mark. No colour emphasis. Just the number.
06
CTA language · the words on the primary button
"Book Now" "Reserve Your Seat" "Learn More" / "Explore"
"Book Now" is e-commerce language. "Reserve Your Seat" is airline language. Both position the interaction as a transaction. For a $300,000 purchase decision, transactional language signals that OrbitX does not understand its customer. Arjun is not buying a product. He is requesting access to a programme.
"Reserve your manifest position →" "Request a briefing →" "Speak to a Specialist"
Mission language throughout. "Manifest position" — not seat. "Briefing" — not consultation. "Specialist" — not sales rep. Every CTA word was chosen to match Arjun's register: a person who has built companies, reads mission documents, and expects to speak to someone who knows more than he does. The arrow is directional, not urgent.
07
Contact mechanism · how the enquiry is handled
Generic contact form. Name / Email / Phone / Message. "We'll be in touch" — no person named.
Generic contact forms communicate that the company does not yet know who is contacting them. For a $300,000 purchase, this signals either that the company is not selective about its passengers or that the enquiry will be handled by someone without authority. Arjun will not fill in a form addressed to nobody.
Three fields: name · contact time · mission. Named specialist: Priya Sharma. "No call centre. No automated response."
A named person with a specific role and a stated number of passengers supported. Three fields only — no email asked (captured from metadata), no phone asked (she calls him). "No deposit required to begin the conversation" removes the last friction point. Arjun is not submitting an enquiry. He is requesting a briefing from a decision-maker.
08
Page structure · the logic of the information sequence
Hero → About / Mission → Experience → Testimonials → Book / Contact
The standard hospitality and luxury product IA: atmosphere first, product second, social proof third, conversion last. This structure works when the customer needs to be convinced. It requires engagement to be earned before information is given. For Arjun, who has already decided in principle, this structure wastes his 20 minutes.
Hero → Vehicle → Passengers → Mission → Training → Manifest → Specialist
A mission briefing document, not a hospitality experience. Each section answers a specific question in Arjun's head at 11pm on a laptop: Is this real? Can I trust the engineering? Has anyone like me done this? What exactly happens? How do I begin? The page does not build atmosphere before substance — it leads with facts and lets atmosphere emerge from precision.
09
Visual direction · the overall palette and image treatment
Dark background. White or blue typography. Vehicle photography — rockets, engines. No consistent colour grading across images.
All three competitors use dark backgrounds and vehicle photography — which is the correct category signal. But the images are used inconsistently in colour temperature and mood. The category look is present but undisciplined. The visual system communicates aerospace but not a specific, considered brand within that category.
Deep Space #020916 base. Three images graded to OrbitX palette: brightness(0.82-0.88) hue-rotate(-15 to -28deg)
Every image graded to the same colour temperature. The warmest image (golden dusk rocket) received the most aggressive grade. The coolest (interior, already blue) the gentlest. A blue multiply overlay on every image. The result: three source images from completely different colour worlds read as a single, consistent visual system. Consistency in tone — not uniformity in temperature.
03 — What these nine decisions add up to

The category builds rockets.
OrbitX builds passengers.

Each of the nine decisions above traces to a specific finding in the research, persona, or competitor audit. None were made by taste. The before column is not wrong — it is appropriate for engineering companies selling missions to institutional clients. The after column is built for Arjun: a founder at 11pm who has already decided in principle and needs confirmation, not persuasion.

The cumulative effect of nine research-led decisions
Blue Origin builds the rocket. Virgin Galactic builds the experience. SpaceX builds the future. OrbitX builds the passenger. Every design decision on this page — alignment, copy, CTA language, IA structure, image grading — points to the same thesis: the person who goes is more important than the vehicle that takes them.
