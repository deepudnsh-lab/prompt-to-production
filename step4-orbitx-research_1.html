01 — Market size & growth

A $1.84B market
expanding at warp speed.

Space tourism is no longer science fiction. It is a commercially active, rapidly expanding industry with three operating suborbital providers, a functioning orbital tourism market, and a forecast trajectory that puts it at $62.1 billion by 2036. The design challenge for OrbitX is not to make space feel real — it already is. The challenge is to make the aspiration feel personal and accessible to the person who can actually afford a seat.

Why suborbital dominates: Suborbital flights — brief trips to the edge of space lasting 10–15 minutes — represent the largest segment because they require less training, less technical risk, and significantly lower ticket prices than orbital missions. Blue Origin's New Shepard and Virgin Galactic's Delta spacecraft both operate in this segment. OrbitX is positioned as a suborbital operator targeting the high-net-worth civilian market.

02 — Ticket pricing landscape

What a seat to space
actually costs.

Pricing in space tourism spans five orders of magnitude depending on the mission type. The suborbital segment — where OrbitX competes — sits between $200,000 and $600,000 per seat. These are not arbitrary numbers: they reflect the actual operational costs of reusable rockets, safety protocols, training programmes, and the extreme scarcity of available seats.

The suborbital price range is expected to decline to $200,000–$300,000 by 2030 as reusable vehicle technology matures and flight cadence increases (Mordor Intelligence, Jan 2026). This creates a window of opportunity for OrbitX to position itself as the premium suborbital experience before pricing democratises the segment further.

03 — Visual & design trends · tagged by status

How space tourism
looks right now.

Dominant now
Deep space dark mode
Near-black backgrounds with blue or white accent palettes are universal across Blue Origin, SpaceX, and Virgin Galactic. The visual language of actual space — darkness, stars, Earth from above — has become the design default for the category. OrbitX must find a way to be premium within this convention, not generic.
Dominant now
Full-viewport hero photography
All three major operators lead with full-screen Earth-from-space imagery. The curvature of the Earth, the thin blue line of the atmosphere, the darkness of space behind it — this is the universal hero visual. Powerful but becoming expected. OrbitX's opportunity is to make the human inside the craft as prominent as the view outside.
Emerging
Human-scale storytelling
Blue Origin's recent all-female crew mission (including Katy Perry, April 2025) generated significantly more media coverage than previous technical missions. The industry is learning that the traveller's story — who they are, why they went, what they saw — is more compelling than the spacecraft's specifications. Design is beginning to follow this shift.
Emerging
Countdown culture
Mission countdown timers, launch windows, reservation queues — the temporal language of space missions is being adopted as a conversion mechanic in web design. The scarcity signal is real: Virgin Galactic has a 700+ person waitlist for its Delta vehicle. "Your seat is waiting" framing is beginning to appear across competitor sites.
Worth watching
Environmental narrative tension
Carbon emissions from rocket launches are drawing increasing scrutiny. Some potential customers are pausing purchases on environmental grounds. Balloon-based operators (Space Perspective, Eos X Space) are positioning against this. Rocket-based operators have not yet addressed it directly in their web design or marketing. A gap that will need to close.
Worth watching
Spaceport as destination
Spaceport America in New Mexico, Mojave in California, and planned locations in Italy and the UAE are being designed as luxury hospitality experiences in their own right. The pre-launch experience — training, medical checks, overnight stays — is becoming a product. Web design for space tourism is beginning to incorporate destination content alongside mission content.
Dominant now
Technical credibility signals
Safety data, vehicle specifications, flight history, mission success rates — all major operators display this information prominently. Unlike luxury hospitality where credentials are implied, space tourism requires explicit proof. The customer needs to trust the rocket before they trust the brand. OrbitX's design must earn technical credibility first.
Emerging
Asia-Pacific market awakening
China's Deep Blue Aerospace sold suborbital tickets for 2027 at approximately $211,000 USD (Oct 2024). India's space tourism infrastructure is expanding with ISRO's Gaganyaan human spaceflight programme targeting 2025–2026. Japan's $6.4B Space Strategy Fund signals government commitment. The customer base is globalising — design must speak to cultures beyond the US luxury traveller archetype.
04 — Key design insights for OrbitX

What the research
tells the design.

01
The view is not the product — the person who saw it is
Every competitor shows the same Earth-from-space imagery. The differentiation opportunity is not better space photography — it is better human storytelling. OrbitX's design should foreground the traveller: who goes, what they bring back, what changes when you see the Earth from outside it. The spacecraft is the vehicle; the transformation is the product.
02
Scarcity is real — it must be stated, not manufactured
Virgin Galactic has a 700+ person waitlist. Blue Origin has flown only 58 passengers since 2021. These numbers are not marketing tactics — they are operational facts. OrbitX should communicate seat availability explicitly, as a factual signal rather than urgency language. "12 seats remain on the Q3 2027 manifest" is more powerful than "Limited seats available."
03
The customer is buying credibility before they are buying an experience
At $200,000–$600,000 per seat, the first question any prospect asks is: is this safe? Before they engage with the experience, they need to believe in the vehicle, the team, and the track record. OrbitX's design must answer the safety question before it asks for a reservation. Technical content — vehicle specs, flight history, safety certifications — must appear high on the page, not buried in a dedicated section.
04
The pricing curve is moving — OrbitX sits in a premium window
Suborbital seat prices are expected to decline from $450,000–$600,000 today to $200,000–$300,000 by 2030 as technology matures (Mordor Intelligence, 2026). OrbitX is positioned in the market before that democratisation happens. This is the moment to establish a premium brand identity that will survive lower prices — just as early commercial aviation established airline brand identities that outlasted the era of elite-only travel.
Design opportunity statement · OrbitX
Every competitor in space tourism shows you the view from outside. OrbitX shows you the person who went. The design territory is human transformation — what changes in a person who has seen the Earth from 100km above it — expressed through a visual language that is as technically credible as it is emotionally compelling. Dark, vast, luminous — and unmistakably human.
