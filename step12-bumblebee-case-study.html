01 — The brief

A children's brand that
refuses every trade-off.

Bumblebee World is the third of six hypothetical brands built as part of From Prompt to Production — a self-initiated portfolio series by Deepu Dinesh, Graphic Design Lead with 13 years of experience. The project demonstrates a complete, research-led web design process executed through AI direction: Claude (Anthropic) and Gemini as execution engines, the designer as the creative director and domain expert.

The brief for this brand: design a complete landing page for a hypothetical Indian children's books and toys brand targeting early learners aged 3–6. The buyer is an urban, aspirational Indian mother — not the child. Every design decision must pass two simultaneous tests: the child's engagement test and the parent's Instagram test. Both must pass. Neither alone is enough.

The gap to close
Every competitor in this category makes a choice: beautiful or Indian. Educational or Instagrammable. No competitor has premium design quality AND Indian cultural content AND physical toys AND a gift-ready product at a D2C price point AND a character universe that travels across every touchpoint. Bumblebee World occupies all five simultaneously.
What makes this case study credible
Every research statistic cites a named source and year. Every design decision traces to a specific research finding or persona insight. 02 — The process

Nine steps.
Each one documented.

The full project followed a 14-step framework. Steps 1–3 were strategy-level work completed once for the entire series. Steps 4–12 were executed specifically for Bumblebee World. Every step produced a standalone HTML document.

01
Project Brief Series level
The big idea, the 14-step framework, the tools, the scope. The rule that nothing unverifiable may be claimed — established here and applied to every subsequent document.
02
Industry Scoring Matrix Series level
16 industries scored against 4 criteria: Visual diversity, Persona clarity, Design challenge, Emotional range. Kids EdTech selected — chosen for its dual-audience design problem and emotional range contrast with OrbitX.
03
Brand Naming Exercise Series level
4 candidates per brand. Bumblebee World won on compound naming with a 14/15 score — both words grounded in physical and emotional truth. "Bumblebee" for the child. "World" for the scope. The double-B sound is naturally playful and easy for a 3-year-old to say.
04
Deep Industry Research Brand level
Global children's book market ($16.4B — Statista 2026), educational toy market ($118B — Grand View Research 2026), India-specific figures (₹4,200 Cr books, ₹18,500 Cr toys), 62% gifting purchase rate, and 6 visual design trends tagged by status.
05
Customer Persona — Priya Sharma Brand level
32 · Bangalore · Product Manager · Mother of Aarav (4) · HHI ₹22 LPA · 45 min Instagram daily · 20 minutes to make a purchase decision. The buyer thesis: Priya does not buy toys. She buys what kind of mother she is.
06
Competitor Visual Audit Brand level
Usborne (19/25 — design quality benchmark, zero Indian content), Kiddopia (15/25 — Indian culture, digital-only, not giftable), Tulika Books (13/25 — deepest cultural content, S:1/5 shareability). The universal gap: no competitor is beautiful AND Indian AND giftable AND Instagrammable simultaneously.
07
Moodboard — 3 Directions Brand level
Direction A (Sunny/Bloom — warm Indian yellow, marigold, Nunito 900) · Direction B (Wild/Lush — deep forest green, Panchatantra animals) · Direction C (Sweet/Petal — rose pink, gifting-first). Direction A selected: most commercially confident, passes both the Instagram test and the gifting test simultaneously.
08
Content & Copywriting Brand level
All copy written before any layout decision. Seven sections, each answering one of Priya's questions in order. Six voice rules. Hero headline: "Every child is a world." Two CTAs: "Start the collection →" for the everyday buyer and "Find the perfect gift" for the gifting buyer — never collapsed into one.
09
Landing Page — Multi-tool build Key step
Seven iterations across Claude (cloud dive experiment, reference image rebuild) before pivoting to a Gemini-generated HTML file with forest illustration system and illustrated book covers — then brought back to Claude for brand alignment, palette correction, image embedding (5 images, ~1.3MB base64), and responsive fixes.
03 — Key design decisions

The choices that
define the brand.

Every design decision in this project traces back to a specific research finding, persona insight, or competitor gap. The four most consequential decisions are documented below.

Decision 01 · Step 05
The dual-audience rule — every element must pass two tests simultaneously
Persona

The Step 5 persona established the defining constraint for the entire brand: Priya buys, Aarav uses. The website must speak to Priya's values (developmental benefit, gifting quality, cultural pride, Instagram shareability) while being visually joyful enough for a 3-year-old to engage with. Every design decision — palette, copy, layout, illustration style, CTA language — was evaluated against both tests before being confirmed. A bright yellow palette passes both: it excites the child and photographs beautifully for the parent. A generic stock image of a white child fails both: it neither excites Aarav nor gives Priya pride. The dual-audience rule is not a compromise — it is the brand's competitive advantage. No competitor in the audit applies it consistently.

Decision 02 · Step 06
Direction A — Sunny / Bloom · warm Indian yellow as the brand's primary signal
Audit + Moodboard

The Step 4 research identified that the Indian children's market has shifted from pale pastels toward warm, saturated, confident colour. The Step 6 audit confirmed that the competitor with the worst shareability score (Tulika Books, S:1/5) used institutional blue-grey — and the competitor with the best visual score (Usborne, D:5/5) used clean white. Sunshine #FFD000 was chosen because it is the colour of marigolds, school bags, turmeric, and ripe mangoes — immediately culturally legible to an Indian parent without being labelled as Indian. It photographs beautifully against the wooden floors and white walls that dominate Indian parenting Instagram. It is warm without being orange, bright without being garish. Direction B (dark forest green) was rejected specifically because it would have failed the gifting test — grandparents would not reach for a dark green product at a birthday party.

Decision 03 · Step 08
Meera's founding story on the homepage — not the About page
Copy + Persona

The Step 5 persona found that Priya converts from interested browser to loyal buyer when she knows who made the product and why. The standard placement for a founder story is the About page — which Priya will never visit in her 20-minute research window. The decision was made to place Meera's founding story directly on the homepage, in the fifth section, between the toys and the reviews. The founding moment — Meera's daughter asked why none of the children in her books looked like her — is also Priya's own unspoken question about Aarav. The story does not need to explain itself. Priya reads it and the connection is made instantly. A founder story on the homepage is a trust signal. A founder story on the About page is a page nobody reads.

Decision 04 · Steps 09 + 10
The multi-tool pivot — Gemini for illustrations, Claude for brand alignment
Workflow

After seven landing page iterations in Claude, the decision was made to bring in a Gemini-generated HTML file that had a forest illustration system and illustrated book cover mockups at a quality level Claude's CSS had not reached. This was not a failure of the process — it was a creative direction decision. The designer's job is to find the best output by any means available. The Gemini file was structurally excellent (forest parallax, illustrated cards, dark/light theme toggle) and content-wrong everywhere else (wrong brand name, wrong palette, wrong copy, API-dependent images). Claude then corrected all content errors, remapped the palette from Direction B dark green to Direction A Sunny/Bloom, embedded all five images as base64, and fixed responsive behaviour. The two-tool workflow produced a better result than either tool alone would have.

05 — What this demonstrates

The skill
being shown.

Dual-audience design thinking
The hardest design problem in the children's category is that the buyer and the user are different people with completely different decision criteria. Bumblebee World is designed to excite a 3-year-old and earn a mother's trust and Instagram post simultaneously. No competitor in the audit achieves both. The discipline required to hold both audiences in every design decision — without compromising either — is the primary skill this case study demonstrates.
Multi-tool workflow fluency
Brand 03 introduced a workflow that Brands 01 and 02 did not use: a Gemini-generated HTML file brought into Claude for brand alignment and correction. The decision to switch tools mid-project was a creative direction decision, not a failure. Knowing when a different tool produces better output — and knowing exactly how to correct what it gets wrong — is a design lead skill, not a technical one. The multi-tool workflow is documented in full in Step 10.
Research-led design — every decision has a source
The dark palette came from the trend analysis in Step 4. The gifting pathway prominence came from the 62% figure in Step 4. The founder story placement came from Priya's conversion triggers in Step 5. The cultural content strategy came from the Kiddopia/Tulika gap in Step 6. Nothing was decided by taste alone. A designer who can read market research and translate it into specific, defensible design decisions is more valuable than one who produces beautiful work without knowing why.
Hypothetical but defensible
Bumblebee World does not exist. But the market it sits in does. The persona it targets does. The competitor landscape it navigates does. Every statistic has a named source and year. Every design decision has a documented rationale. If a hiring manager asks why the CTA says "Start the collection" and not "Buy Now," the answer is in Step 5. If they ask why the yellow palette was chosen over the green, the answer is in Step 7. The case study is bulletproof because the process was honest.
