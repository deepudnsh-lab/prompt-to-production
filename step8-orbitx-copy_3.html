01 — Page structure · information architecture

Eight sections.
Eight questions answered.

The OrbitX page is structured like a mission briefing document — not a brochure, not a campaign. Each section exists because it answers a specific question in the reader's mind. If a section cannot be reduced to the question it answers, it does not belong on the page.

The sequence follows the logic of a high-value technical decision: credibility first, proof second, detail third, commitment last. The page does not build atmosphere before substance. It leads with facts and lets atmosphere emerge from precision.

The structural principle: Arjun is at 11pm in a hotel room with 20 minutes. Every section must earn his continued attention by answering the next question in his head. The moment a section fails to do that, he closes the tab.

01
Navigation Anchor
Fixed · wordmark · 4 section labels · primary CTA
The four nav labels — The Vehicle, The Mission, The Training, The Manifest — read like a classified document's table of contents. Arjun reads nav labels before the hero. These four words tell him immediately what kind of company OrbitX is. The CTA is "Speak to a Specialist" — not Book, not Reserve, not Contact Us.
02
Hero — Credential Statement Anchor
Full viewport · helmet visor image · headline + four mission stats
Answers: "Is this real?" The hero does not sell the experience — it states the facts. Altitude, duration, passenger count, next mission designation. The headline is bold and factual. The image is a human eye reflected in a visor with Earth behind it. The passenger is the subject, not the rocket.
03
The Vehicle — Technical Credentials
Dark section · philosophy statement + 4-cell spec grid
Answers: "Can I trust the engineering?" Vehicle name, altitude ceiling, propulsion type, reusability status. This section appears before any experience content because the reader will not emotionally engage until he trusts the machine. The philosophy statement — "We do not launch until we are certain" — appears here, not on the homepage hero.
04
Previous Passengers — Human Proof Proof
Dark section · 3 anonymised biographical signal cards
Answers: "Has anyone like me done this?" Not testimonials — biographical signals. Founder, Mumbai, flew March 2027. Chairman, Dubai, flew August 2027. Passenger identities kept confidential by request. The reader sees his peer group. He does not need a quote. He needs a mirror.
05
The Mission — What Actually Happens
Light section · T-minus timeline · 6 cards from medical clearance to landing
Answers: "What exactly am I buying?" A precise sequence — not experience language. T-72hrs: medical clearance. T-48hrs: arrive at spaceport. T-24hrs: simulator and briefing. T-0: launch. T+11min: apogee. T+22min: touchdown. This is an itinerary, not a narrative.
06
The Training — Pre-Flight Programme
Light section · 4-card two-day programme · presented as premium itinerary
Answers: "What do I have to do before I go?" Presented as a premium two-day programme — not a requirement. Medical assessment, full mission simulation, vehicle walk-around, suit fitting, crew dinner. By the time the passenger boards, they have already experienced everything once.
07
The Manifest — Mission Windows Convert
Dark section · 3 active missions · specific seat counts · no urgency language
Answers: "How do I secure a position?" Real mission designations, specific dates, actual seat counts. Not manufactured scarcity — operational fact. Three missions listed. The reader can see the pipeline and choose his window. The CTA is "Reserve your manifest position" — not Book Now.
08
Speak to a Specialist — Contact Convert
Dark section · 3-field form · named Asia-Pacific specialist
Answers: "Who do I actually speak to?" A named mission specialist — name, title, number of passengers supported. Three fields only: name, preferred contact time, mission of interest. No deposit required to begin the conversation. The reader is not submitting an enquiry. He is requesting a briefing.
02 — Voice & tone · six rules

The voice is a briefing.
Not a brochure.

OrbitX copy is aerospace precision with human warmth held just below the surface. It does not try to impress. It informs. The emotion lives in the facts — not in the adjectives. Every line should feel like it was written by someone who has actually been there.

Rule 01
Facts before feelings
Write: "100km. 11 minutes. 6 passengers."
Not: "An extraordinary journey to the edge of the universe."
Rule 02
Never use superlatives
Write: "The most capable suborbital vehicle currently operating."
Not: "The world's most incredible spacecraft."
Rule 03
Specific over vague
Write: "4 of 6 seats reserved · NX-14 · Q2 2028."
Not: "Limited availability. Reserve early."
Rule 04
The passenger is always the subject
Write: "At 100km, you see the planet."
Not: "OrbitX takes you to the edge of space."
Rule 05
Inform. Never sell.
Write: "The manifest for NX-14 is open."
Not: "Don't miss your chance. Book now."
Rule 06
Sentences end. They do not trail.
Write: "You will not see Earth the same way again."
Not: "An experience that will change you forever…"

The emotion arrives from contrast, not from decoration.
Medical clearance geography
Partner facilities in Mumbai, Dubai, and Singapore are named explicitly in the first card. This removes a friction point before the reader even considers it. He does not need to travel abroad for a physical. OrbitX meets him where he is. One fewer reason to pause. One more signal that this brand understands exactly who its customer is.

Light section · 4 cards · two-day programme as premium itinerary
The Training
Two days.
Everything you need.
The OrbitX pre-flight programme is not a requirement. It is the beginning of the mission. By the time you board, you will have met your crew, flown the simulation, and understood exactly what to expect. Most passengers consider it the second-best part of the experience.
Day 01 · Arrival
Medical & crew introduction
Physiological assessment. Meet your five fellow passengers and flight commander. Facility orientation. Overnight at the spaceport lodge.
Day 02 · Morning
Full mission simulation
Complete mission sequence in the full-motion simulator. Launch forces, weightlessness, re-entry. Every moment, experienced once before the real thing.
Day 02 · Afternoon
Vehicle walk-around & suit fitting
Full access to the NX vehicle on the pad. Suit fitted by the same technician who will suit you at T-0. Final questions with the flight surgeon.
Day 02 · Evening
Crew dinner & final briefing
Private crew dinner at the spaceport facility. Final mission briefing. Early lights-out. Tomorrow you launch.
Reframe decision
"Most passengers consider it the second-best part of the experience." One sentence reframes the entire training from obligation to highlight. It also communicates something important: OrbitX has enough previous passengers to have observed patterns. This is not a prototype. It is a programme with a track record.
Final line
"Tomorrow you launch." Four words. No fuss, no exclamation, no dramatisation. The restraint is the emotion. The reader processes those four words and feels the weight of what is coming — not because they were amplified, but because they were stated plainly. That is the OrbitX voice at its most precise.

Scarcity copy
"2 of 6 seats remaining." Not "Only 2 left!" The number is a fact, not a pressure mechanism. The reader has spent his career distinguishing between genuine scarcity and manufactured urgency. The plain number — without exclamation, without italics, without colour emphasis — is more convincing than any urgency framing because it respects his intelligence.
Friction removal
"No deposit required to begin the conversation." This removes the final remaining commitment anxiety. The reader is not being asked to transfer $300,000 via a button. He is being invited to speak to a specialist. The financial commitment happens between humans — not between a human and a payment form. That distinction matters enormously at this price point.
