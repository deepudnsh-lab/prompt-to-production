01 — Why copy comes before layout

Words decide
the design.

Most designers reach for layout before they have the words. The result is placeholder copy that shapes the layout, and then real copy that never quite fits. In this project, copy comes first — every headline, every menu description, every CTA, every scarcity signal is written before a single layout decision is made. The layout in Step 9 will be built around these exact words.

Each section of copy below is shown in two ways: a live dark-mode preview in the Noir direction's actual typefaces and colours, and a written annotation explaining what the copy is doing and why every word was chosen. Nothing is accidental. Rohit — the persona — is the filter for every decision.

The copy rules for Ember & Oak: Specific over superlative. Sensory over declarative. Short over long. Every claim must be provable or observable. The word "best" never appears. The words "hickory," "apple," "cedar," and "camphor" appear often. The scarcity signal ("28 guests each evening") appears at least twice. The CTA is always "Reserve a Table" — never "Book Now."

02 — Voice principles

How Ember & Oak
speaks.

Principle 01
Specific, not superlative
✗ "The finest wood-fire dining in Bangalore"

✓ "Hickory for the lamb. Apple for the sea bass. Cedar for the mushrooms."
Principle 02
Sensory, not descriptive
✗ "A unique dining experience featuring premium ingredients"

✓ "The smoke arrives before the plate. That is intentional."
Principle 03
Unhurried, not urgent
✗ "Book your table today! Limited slots available!"

✓ "Twenty-eight seats. One sitting each evening. The fire is lit at four."
Principle 04
Short sentences. Full stops.
✗ "At Ember & Oak, we believe that the finest dining experiences are those that honour both the ingredient and the flame that transforms it."

✓ "The ingredient. The flame. The time between."
Principle 05
Confident, not apologetic
✗ "We hope you'll enjoy our carefully crafted seasonal menu"

✓ "The menu changes when the season does. Not before."
Principle 06
The wood is always named
✗ "Slow-cooked over open flame with premium wood"

✓ "Slow-cooked over hickory for six hours. No glaze. No shortcut."

Copy decisions
Navigation labels are "The fire / The menu / The experience" rather than generic "About / Menu / Book." The definite article "The" signals that these are not categories but things that exist with intention — Ember & Oak's fire, menu, and experience, not any fire or menu. The CTA is "Reserve a Table" — not "Book Now" or "Book a Table." "Reserve" is quieter and more considered. It implies the table is already set.
What Rohit reads from this
The navigation structure tells him immediately that this restaurant has a point of view — it organises itself around what it does (fire, menu, experience) rather than what it is. "Reserve a Table" rather than "Book Now" signals unhurried confidence rather than e-commerce urgency. Both signals contribute to the 5-second trust test.

Copy decisions
Eyebrow: "28 guests each evening" appears here at the first viewport — not buried below. This is the scarcity signal. It does not feel like a marketing tactic because it is presented as factual information, not urgency. Headline: "Where fire meets flavour" is the tagline as a statement. The em dash and italic on "flavour" creates rhythm and draws the eye. Sub-copy: Three words then a sentence. "Wood-fired. Seasonal. Unhurried." — the full stops make each word carry its own weight. The fire timing ("lit at four, dinner at seven") is specific and visual. Strip: The wood types at the bottom are the most distinctive copy on the page — no competitor does this. They are labels, not sentences, and they are always specific.
What Rohit reads from this
Within the first 5 seconds Rohit knows: it's in Bangalore, it opened in 2026, only 28 people eat here per sitting, it's wood-fired, and the specific wood varies by protein. This is more information than most fine dining sites give him in the entire homepage — and none of it is a superlative. His product-trained brain responds to specificity. He trusts this immediately because it is showing, not telling.
Section 03 · The fire — philosophy Second section · Narrative · No CTA
The fire
Not every flame
cooks the same way.
Hickory burns slow and dense — it is for proteins that need time. Apple burns clean and sweet — it is for delicate fish that must not be overwhelmed. Cedar carries a resinous depth that belongs only to the mushroom course. Camphor smokes from a distance and never touches the food directly.

Every wood is chosen before the menu is written. Not after.
Copy decisions
Headline: Starts with "Not" — a confident negation that immediately sets up a distinction. It implies that other restaurants use fire without thinking about it. Ember & Oak does. Body copy: Four woods, four sentences, each structured identically (wood — property — application). The parallel structure makes this feel like a list of principles, not marketing copy. The final line — "Every wood is chosen before the menu is written. Not after." — is the most important sentence on the page. It tells Rohit this restaurant thinks backwards from the method, not forward from the ingredient.
What Rohit reads from this
This is the section that separates Ember & Oak from every other wood-fire restaurant he has encountered. Naming specific woods and their culinary rationale is the kind of specificity that Rohit — a product person — reads as depth of craft. It tells him the kitchen is led by someone who thinks carefully about process. This earns the premium price point before he has even seen the menu.

Copy decisions
Menu label: "Spring 2026" — makes the seasonal commitment visible. It tells Rohit this menu will be different when he comes back. Descriptions: Short, precise, never more than four ingredients listed. The wood type appears as a right-aligned label for every dish — this is the design detail that makes the philosophy visible in the menu. What Rohit reads from this
This is the first thing he looks at. The wood type on every dish makes the philosophy section above feel earned rather than promotional — it is literally applied to every item. The descriptions are short enough that he reads all of them in under a minute. "Deccan Lamb Rack · slow-cooked six hours · hickory" tells him more about the kitchen's approach than a paragraph of brand copy ever could. "Not on the menu" for the amuse bouche makes him want to come and find out.
Section 05 · The experience — scarcity signal Fourth section · Converts intent to reservation · Key urgency moment
The experience
Twenty-eight seats.
One sitting.
Every evening.
There is no second sitting. No walk-ins. The kitchen does not scale. It simply does the same thing, at the same hour, for the same number of people — and it does it very well.
28
guests each evening · one chef's table for six · one private dining room for eight · all other tables for two or four · reservations required
Copy decisions
Headline: Three short lines, each a complete statement. The rhythm is deliberate — each line lands separately, like steps. "Every evening" as the italic close creates finality. Body: "There is no second sitting. No walk-ins. The kitchen does not scale." — three negations in a row, each removing an expectation of a lesser restaurant. The final sentence — "it simply does the same thing, at the same hour, for the same number of people — and it does it very well" — is the most confident line in the entire document. The understatement ("very well") is more powerful than any superlative. Scarcity block: The number 28 rendered large is the design and the copy simultaneously.
What Rohit reads from this
This is the section that converts him from browser to booker. The scarcity is not manufactured — it is presented as fact, as operational reality. "The kitchen does not scale" tells him this is not a business decision but a culinary one. For Rohit, who values craft over convenience, this framing is exactly right. The 28 rendered large is the moment he realises this is genuinely hard to get. He opens the reservation form.

Copy decisions
Headline: "Your table. When you're ready." — the possessive "your" is doing important work here. It pre-assigns the table, making the act of booking feel like claiming something rather than requesting it. "When you're ready" is unhurried — no urgency, no scarcity tactic. Body: The "dietary requirements" line is considerate and specific. "The kitchen will be informed" — five words that communicate end-to-end care without promising anything ornate. Form: Four fields only. Date, guests, name, contact. Nothing more. CTA: "Confirm Reservation" rather than "Book" or "Submit." Confirm implies it was already yours — this finalises it.
What Rohit reads from this
The headline lands the scarcity established in Section 05 — the table is already implicitly his, he just needs to confirm it. Four fields is the correct number — he fills it in without friction. "Cancellations accepted up to 48 hours" removes risk from the decision, making it easier to commit. The form CTA "Confirm Reservation" is the final copy moment — it closes the loop between "I want to go" and "I am going."

Copy decisions
The footer repeats the three most important factual signals from the page — the fire timing, the dinner time, and the cover count — as a closing line. This is the last thing Rohit reads. It reinforces everything. The address is on Lavelle Road — Bangalore's premium dining corridor — which signals neighbourhood quality without stating it. "Dinner only · 7 pm" communicates a single-minded focus. "Monday closed" signals a kitchen that rests, not a business that never stops.
What Rohit reads from this
The closing line — "The fire is lit at four · Dinner begins at seven · Twenty-eight guests each evening" — is the brand in three phrases. It is both a statement of operations and a statement of identity. If Rohit reads nothing else on this page, he reads this, and he understands what Ember & Oak is. This is the test for any footer: can it carry the whole brand in one line?
