01 — The brief

A self-serve AI analytics product
built for one person.

NeuralDesk is the fourth of six hypothetical brands built as part of From Prompt to Production — a self-initiated portfolio series by Deepu Dinesh, Graphic Design Lead with 13 years of experience. The project demonstrates a complete, research-led web design process executed through AI direction. Claude (Anthropic) is the execution engine. The designer is the creative director.

The brief: design a complete landing page for a hypothetical self-serve AI data analytics SaaS product. The buyer is Marcus Webb — a 33-year-old Product Manager at a Series B startup in Austin, TX. He has one analyst on his team, always at capacity. He needs answers from data today. He will sign up in the same session he discovers the product — or not at all.

The gap to close
The AI analytics category is bifurcated: enterprise tools with high design quality and no self-serve access (Tableau, ThoughtSpot) versus self-serve tools with low design quality and full access (Looker Studio, Polymer). No product at this moment occupies ThoughtSpot-quality design at a self-serve price point — with pricing visible on the homepage and no demo gate.

Every market statistic has a named source and year. Every design decision traces to a specific research finding or persona insight. 02 — The insight that drove everything

The landing page
is the sales team.

The Step 5 persona established the most important constraint for the entire product: Marcus Webb makes his purchase decision in the same session he discovers NeuralDesk — or not at all. There is no follow-up call. There is no demo to handle objections. The landing page must do everything a sales team would normally do, in the time it takes Marcus to read seven sections on a Wednesday afternoon with three competitor tabs open.

The central design thesis · NeuralDesk
Tableau has the trust. Looker Studio has the access. ThoughtSpot has the design. NeuralDesk is the first to have all three — at a price point Marcus can approve without a meeting, with an onboarding flow he can complete before his next standup.
Marcus does not want a platform
He wants the answer to a specific question he has right now. The hero headline "One question. One answer. Now." is not a tagline — it is a direct response to the pain identified in Step 5: three hours per week lost waiting for a report that should take 60 seconds. Every word on the landing page was written to speak to that specific frustration.
Skepticism is Marcus's default state
Marcus has been burned by tools that promised "AI-powered insights" and delivered rebranded pivot tables. The landing page never claims AI as a feature. It shows a specific query, a specific result, a specific metric. The product UI demonstrated on the hero panel is more persuasive than any feature description — because Marcus can see exactly what he would type and exactly what he would get back.

04 — Four key design decisions

The choices that
define the brand.

Decision 01 · Step 05
The seven-question IA — every section answers Marcus's next question
Persona

The Step 5 persona revealed that Marcus evaluates three tools simultaneously in three browser tabs. The landing page that wins is the one that communicates its value fastest — because he is context-switching constantly. The IA was designed as Marcus's sequential evaluation session: What does this do? → Is it legitimate? → What do I actually do? → What will the output look like? → Has someone like me used this? → Can I afford this? → How do I start? Each section answers exactly one question — no more. Pricing is the sixth section because Marcus must see the product demonstrated before he'll read the price. The final CTA section has one button — not two — because if Marcus has scrolled through all seven sections, he is ready. A second CTA at that point introduces doubt.

Decision 02 · Step 07
Direction C — Arctic / Clarity · pure red on white · the most minimal direction
Moodboard

The three moodboard directions were genuinely distinct: Direction A (Bloomberg-meets-SaaS, authoritative, tight) · Direction B (editorial serif, warm, startup energy) · Direction C (Linear/Vercel register, ultra-minimal, wide-tracked, maximum white space). Direction C was selected because it is the only direction in the category that no competitor currently occupies. Tableau and ThoughtSpot are dark and technical. Looker Studio is light but generic Material. No product at this price point has the restraint, the typographic precision, and the confident white-space approach of Direction C. The pure red accent — used on CTAs, data highlights, and the brand mark only — earns its full visual weight because it appears so rarely.

Decision 03 · Step 09
The hero UI — four full dashboard screens, tab-switched, not a screenshot
Landing page

Most SaaS landing pages show a static product screenshot in the hero — a frozen image of the UI that communicates nothing about how the product actually works. The NeuralDesk hero shows four complete, functional dashboard screens — Overview, Funnel Analysis, Channel Report, and Retention Cohorts — each with real metric cards, SVG charts, and a contextual query bar at the bottom with an animated typewriter cycling through five real PM questions. Marcus can click each tab and see exactly which questions the product answers. The UI is built in CSS — no images required — which means it loads instantly and scales correctly at any viewport size. The dashboard demonstrates the product; it does not merely illustrate it.

Decision 04 · Step 08
Visible pricing with a "justify to your manager" one-pager link
Copy + Persona

The Step 4 research confirmed that Tableau, Power BI, and ThoughtSpot all hide pricing behind a demo gate. The Step 5 persona confirmed that "Contact sales for pricing" is an immediate exit for Marcus. Pricing is visible on the NeuralDesk homepage. Three tiers — Free ($0), Pro ($29/month), Team ($79/month) — with the Pro tier highlighted in red because it is the one Marcus will choose. The most consequential line on the pricing section is not the price: it is "Need to justify this to your manager? Generate a one-pager →" The Team plan at $79/month requires Marcus to send a message to his manager. That link generates the ammunition he needs for that message — removing the last conversion barrier between solo approval and team adoption.

06 — What this demonstrates

The skill
being shown.

Research producing design decisions
The pricing transparency, the outcome-led copy, the non-technical buyer language, the light theme at premium quality — none of these were aesthetic preferences. Every decision traces to a specific finding in the Step 4 research or the Step 5 persona. The design is defensible because the process was honest. If a hiring manager asks why the CTA says "Start free" and not "Get a demo," the answer is in the Step 5 persona document. If they ask why the pricing is on the homepage, the answer is in the Step 4 competitor table.
Technical execution across the stack
The landing page is a single HTML file — no framework, no build tooling, no CDN. Four complete dashboard UIs built entirely in CSS and SVG. Animated typewriter. Scroll-reveal system. Tab switcher. Hamburger menu. Responsive at every breakpoint from 320px to 1440px. Custom cursor. The technical execution demonstrates that AI direction can produce production-quality frontend work — not a proof of concept, not a wireframe, but a deployable file.
Category intelligence as design strategy
NeuralDesk's landing page is structurally different from every competitor because the Step 4 research identified specifically what every competitor does and where every competitor fails. "No competitor has both premium design AND self-serve access AND visible pricing" — that gap, confirmed by the Step 6 audit, produced the entire brand positioning. The competitor audit is not background research. It is the brief.
Hypothetical but portfolio-grade
NeuralDesk does not exist. Marcus Webb is a composite persona. But the market it sits in does. The buyer it targets does. The competitor landscape it navigates does. Every statistic has a named source and year. Every design decision has a document. The case study is bulletproof because the process was documented in real time, not reconstructed after the fact. This is what separates portfolio work from personal projects.
